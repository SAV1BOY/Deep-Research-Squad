# Literature Gap Mapping Framework

## Purpose

Identify gaps in the existing literature. Determine what questions remain unanswered, where evidence is weak or contradictory, and where entire subtopics have been neglected. Gap mapping transforms a literature review from a summary of what is known into a strategic guide for what still needs to be investigated.

## When to Use

- After completing a systematic review or extensive literature scan.
- When planning a new research project and need to identify original contributions.
- When advising on research priorities or funding allocation.
- When a field appears mature but may have blind spots.
- When conflicting findings suggest unresolved methodological or conceptual issues.

## Inputs

- A completed literature review or systematic review with extracted data.
- A taxonomy or categorization of the field's key topics and subtopics.
- Knowledge of the field's major research questions and debates.
- Understanding of stakeholder needs and practical applications.

## Process

1. **Build the topic matrix.** Create a matrix with key topics on one axis and research dimensions on the other (e.g., populations studied, methods used, geographies, time periods, contexts).
2. **Map existing literature onto the matrix.** Place each reviewed source into the relevant cells. Count the density of coverage per cell.
3. **Identify empty cells.** Cells with no coverage represent absolute gaps: questions no one has addressed.
4. **Identify thin cells.** Cells with only one or two sources represent fragile evidence: findings that have not been validated or replicated.
5. **Flag contradictory cells.** Cells where sources disagree represent unresolved debates requiring further investigation.
6. **Assess methodological gaps.** Are certain methods overrepresented? Has the topic only been studied with surveys but never with experiments? Only with case studies but never at scale?
7. **Check temporal gaps.** Has research stalled? Are the most recent studies years old? Has the context changed since the last study?
8. **Evaluate population gaps.** Has the topic only been studied in certain populations, industries, or geographies? Would findings generalize?
9. **Prioritize gaps.** Rank gaps by importance (how much does this gap matter?) and feasibility (can this gap be addressed with available methods and data?).
10. **Formulate gap statements.** Write clear, specific statements for each significant gap, suitable for motivating future research.

## Outputs

- A topic matrix showing coverage density across dimensions.
- A prioritized list of identified gaps with clear descriptions.
- An assessment of evidence strength per subtopic.
- Specific research questions that would address the most important gaps.
- Recommendations for methods and approaches to fill priority gaps.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Mistaking absence of search for absence of research.** A gap in your review may reflect incomplete searching, not a true gap.
- **Overvaluing novelty.** Not every gap is worth filling; some questions are unimportant.
- **Ignoring grey literature.** Gaps may appear in academic literature but be addressed in industry reports, theses, or government documents.
- **Static mapping.** The map reflects a point in time; new research may fill gaps after the map is created.
- **Treating all gaps equally.** Some gaps are critical; others are trivial. Prioritization is essential.
- **Confusing a gap with a negative finding.** If researchers looked and found nothing, that is a finding, not a gap.

## Quality Criteria

- The topic matrix must have at least two dimensions and cover the full scope of the field as defined by the taxonomy.
- Every gap must be classified by type: absolute gap (no coverage), thin evidence (1-2 sources), or contradictory evidence.
- Prioritization must consider both importance and feasibility, not just one dimension.
- Gap statements must be specific enough to serve as research questions for future investigation.
