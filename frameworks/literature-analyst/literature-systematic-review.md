# Literature Systematic Review Framework

## Purpose

Conduct a structured, reproducible review of existing literature on a topic. Unlike a casual literature scan, a systematic review follows a defined protocol: explicit search criteria, systematic source identification, standardized inclusion and exclusion rules, structured data extraction, and evidence synthesis. The goal is comprehensive, unbiased coverage of what is known.

## When to Use

- When a research question requires understanding the full body of evidence.
- When previous reviews may have been selective or biased.
- When decision-makers need confidence that all relevant evidence has been considered.
- When synthesizing findings across multiple studies, reports, or sources.
- As the foundation for any research project in a mature domain.

## Inputs

- A clearly defined research question or set of questions.
- Access to relevant databases, repositories, and search engines.
- Time and resource budget for the review.
- Domain knowledge sufficient to design search queries and evaluate sources.

## Process

1. **Define the research question.** Use a structured format (e.g., PICO: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) to ensure precision.
2. **Develop the search protocol.** Specify databases, search terms, date ranges, and languages. Document everything for reproducibility.
3. **Execute the search.** Run searches across all specified databases. Record the number of results from each source.
4. **Remove duplicates.** Consolidate results and remove duplicate entries across databases.
5. **Screen by title and abstract.** Apply inclusion and exclusion criteria to titles and abstracts. Document reasons for exclusion.
6. **Screen by full text.** Read remaining sources in full. Apply detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria.
7. **Extract data.** Use a standardized extraction template to pull key data from each included source: methods, findings, sample size, limitations, quality indicators.
8. **Assess source quality.** Rate each source on methodological quality, relevance, and reliability.
9. **Synthesize findings.** Aggregate extracted data. Identify patterns, contradictions, and gaps. Use narrative synthesis or quantitative methods as appropriate.
10. **Document the process.** Produce a flow diagram showing sources at each stage (searched, screened, included, excluded with reasons).

## Outputs

- A completed search protocol document.
- A source flow diagram (searched, screened, included, excluded).
- A data extraction table for all included sources.
- A quality assessment for each included source.
- A narrative or quantitative synthesis of findings.
- Identified gaps, contradictions, and areas needing further research.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Incomplete search.** Missing key databases or using overly narrow search terms.
- **Inconsistent criteria.** Applying inclusion and exclusion rules differently across sources.
- **Publication bias.** Systematic reviews inherit the publication bias of the literature they review.
- **Language bias.** Excluding non-English sources can skew findings.
- **Confirmation bias in synthesis.** Emphasizing findings that support a preferred conclusion.
- **Scope creep.** Expanding the research question mid-review, compromising systematic rigor.
- **Skipping quality assessment.** Treating all sources as equally reliable.

## Quality Criteria

- The search protocol must be documented before execution, not reconstructed afterward.
- Inclusion and exclusion criteria must be applied consistently; ideally, a sample should be double-checked for consistency.
- The source flow must be fully documented: total found, duplicates removed, excluded at title/abstract, excluded at full text, included.
- Quality assessment must use a defined rubric, not ad hoc judgment.
- The synthesis must address contradictions and gaps, not just summarize agreements.
