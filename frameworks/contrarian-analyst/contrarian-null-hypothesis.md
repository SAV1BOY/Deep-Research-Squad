# Contrarian Null Hypothesis Framework

## Purpose

Default to the null hypothesis for every research claim. The burden of proof falls on the claim, not on the skeptic. This framework enforces disciplined skepticism by requiring that every conclusion demonstrate it has overcome the default assumption that nothing unusual, new, or exceptional is happening.

## When to Use

- At the start of any research investigation, to set the evidentiary bar.
- When evaluating claims that something is "new," "unprecedented," or "revolutionary."
- When reviewing research that confirms a popular or desired narrative.
- When the cost of a false positive (accepting a wrong claim) is high.
- When prior probability of the claim being true is low or unknown.

## Inputs

- The research claim or hypothesis to be tested.
- Available evidence for and against the claim.
- Base rate information for similar claims in this domain.
- Context on the consequences of false positives versus false negatives.

## Process

1. **State the null hypothesis.** Formulate what the world looks like if the claim is false. The null is typically: "There is no effect," "There is no difference," or "This is explained by known factors."
2. **State the alternative hypothesis.** This is the research claim itself, precisely formulated.
3. **Define the evidentiary threshold.** What quantity and quality of evidence is needed to reject the null? Set this before examining the evidence.
4. **Catalog all evidence.** List every piece of evidence, noting whether it supports the null, the alternative, or is ambiguous.
5. **Test against mundane explanations.** For each piece of evidence supporting the alternative, ask: Can this be explained without the alternative hypothesis? Consider coincidence, confounding variables, measurement error, and selection bias.
6. **Assess cumulative weight.** Does the totality of evidence clear the threshold set in step 3?
7. **Apply the base rate test.** Given how often similar claims turn out to be true, does this claim have sufficient evidence to overcome the prior?
8. **Render a verdict.** Either reject the null (accept the claim with stated confidence) or fail to reject the null (maintain skepticism, identify what evidence would change the verdict).

## Outputs

- Explicit null and alternative hypotheses.
- The evidentiary threshold and justification for choosing it.
- A catalog of evidence mapped to null versus alternative.
- Assessment of whether the null has been rejected.
- If the null survives: what additional evidence would be needed.
- If the null is rejected: at what confidence level and with what caveats.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Setting the bar after seeing the evidence.** The threshold must be defined before evaluation.
- **Confusing "fail to reject" with "the null is true."** Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
- **Ignoring the null entirely.** Many research efforts never articulate what the null looks like.
- **Moving the goalposts.** Changing the threshold when the evidence is close to the boundary.
- **Asymmetric skepticism.** Applying the null rigorously to claims you dislike but not to claims you favor.
- **Forgetting practical significance.** Statistical significance does not imply real-world importance.
- **Treating the null as the enemy.** The null is a tool for clarity, not an obstacle to overcome.

## Quality Criteria

- The null hypothesis must be stated precisely enough that it could be falsified.
- The evidentiary threshold must be documented before any evidence is examined.
- The evidence catalog must include items supporting both the null and the alternative, with no selective omission.
- The verdict must clearly state whether the null was rejected or not, and at what confidence level.
