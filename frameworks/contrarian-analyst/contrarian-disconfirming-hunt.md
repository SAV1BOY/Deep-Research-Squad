# Contrarian Disconfirming Evidence Hunt Framework

## Purpose

Actively and systematically hunt for evidence that disproves the main thesis. Humans naturally seek confirming evidence (confirmation bias). This framework counteracts that tendency by making disconfirmation the explicit goal of a dedicated research phase. You do not stop at the first confirming evidence; you keep searching until you have exhausted plausible disconfirming sources.

## When to Use

- After the main thesis has been supported by initial evidence gathering.
- When the research feels "too clean" or the evidence feels one-sided.
- Before finalizing any high-stakes research deliverable.
- When the researcher notices they have only searched for supporting evidence.
- As a mandatory step in any structured research workflow.

## Inputs

- The main thesis or conclusion, clearly stated.
- The evidence that currently supports the thesis.
- A list of sources already consulted.
- The domain and topic area for targeted disconfirming searches.

## Process

1. **List the core claims.** Break the thesis into its constituent claims. Each one gets its own disconfirmation search.
2. **Identify what would disprove each claim.** For each claim, define what evidence would make it false or significantly weaker.
3. **Design disconfirming search queries.** Craft searches that would surface opposing evidence. Use terms like "failure," "criticism," "debunked," "limitations," "does not work," "overestimated," and domain-specific negative terms.
4. **Search sources the main research did not consult.** Deliberately look in places that were not part of the original research. Different databases, different communities, different languages, different time periods.
5. **Seek out critics and skeptics.** Find individuals, organizations, or publications known for skepticism in this domain. Read their arguments charitably.
6. **Look for failed replications.** If the thesis rests on studies or experiments, search for replication attempts, especially failed ones.
7. **Check for retracted or corrected sources.** Verify that key supporting sources have not been retracted, corrected, or challenged since publication.
8. **Examine edge cases and exceptions.** Look for cases where the thesis should hold but does not. Exceptions can be more informative than confirmations.
9. **Document everything found.** Record all disconfirming evidence, even if it seems weak. Weak disconfirming evidence in aggregate can be significant.
10. **Assess the damage.** How much does the disconfirming evidence weaken the thesis? Does it require revision, qualification, or abandonment?

## Outputs

- A catalog of disconfirming evidence organized by claim.
- An assessment of the strength and relevance of each disconfirming item.
- A revised thesis that accounts for disconfirming evidence, or a statement of why the thesis stands despite it.
- A list of sources and communities consulted during the hunt.
- Remaining open questions where disconfirming evidence is insufficient to resolve.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Superficial search.** Running one or two searches and declaring no disconfirming evidence exists.
- **Dismissing disconfirming evidence prematurely.** Applying higher scrutiny to disconfirming evidence than to confirming evidence.
- **Source bias.** Only searching in sources that are likely to confirm the thesis.
- **Stopping too early.** The hunt should be proportional to the stakes of the conclusion.
- **Treating the hunt as adversarial.** The goal is truth, not winning a debate.
- **Forgetting to update.** Finding disconfirming evidence but not incorporating it into the final analysis.

## Quality Criteria

- The hunt must cover at least three independent source categories not used in the original research.
- Every core claim must have a dedicated disconfirmation search, not just the overall thesis.
- Disconfirming evidence must be evaluated with the same rigor applied to confirming evidence, no double standards.
- The final assessment must quantify how much the thesis was weakened, using terms like "unchanged," "slightly weakened," "significantly weakened," or "undermined."
