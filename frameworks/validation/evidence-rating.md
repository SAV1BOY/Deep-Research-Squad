# Evidence Rating

## Purpose
A standardized rating system for evaluating the quality of individual pieces of evidence. Uses grades A through F to classify evidence reliability, enabling researchers to weight findings appropriately and communicate confidence levels clearly.

## When to Use
- When evaluating each piece of evidence gathered during research
- When comparing conflicting sources to determine which to trust
- When building arguments that depend on evidence quality
- When communicating findings to decision-makers who need to understand reliability

## Inputs
- The specific claim or finding being evaluated
- The source from which the evidence comes
- Metadata about the source: author, publication, date, methodology
- Context about the domain and what constitutes strong evidence in that field

## Process

### Grade A: Gold Standard
- Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, or large-scale replicated studies
- Official statistics from authoritative agencies with transparent methodology
- Primary documents (contracts, filings, legislation) directly relevant to the claim
- Criteria: multiple independent confirmations, rigorous methodology, full transparency

### Grade B: Strong Evidence
- Peer-reviewed studies with sound methodology but limited replication
- Reports from established institutions with documented methodology
- Multiple credible news sources reporting independently with named sources
- Criteria: clear methodology, credible authorship, some independent confirmation

### Grade C: Adequate Evidence
- Single peer-reviewed study without replication
- Industry reports from reputable firms with some methodology disclosure
- Expert opinion from recognized domain authorities with reasoning provided
- Criteria: credible source, reasonable methodology, limited independent confirmation

### Grade D: Weak Evidence
- Non-peer-reviewed analysis, blog posts from domain practitioners
- Single-source news reports, especially with anonymous sources
- Surveys with unclear methodology or small sample sizes
- Criteria: some credibility but significant gaps in methodology or verification

### Grade E: Speculative
- Opinion pieces, editorials, or commentary without supporting data
- Social media posts, forum discussions, or unverified claims
- Extrapolations far beyond the supporting data
- Criteria: minimal methodological rigor, no independent confirmation, high bias risk

### Grade F: Unreliable
- Sources with known track records of inaccuracy or fabrication
- Circular citations where sources reference each other without independent basis
- Evidence contradicted by multiple higher-grade sources
- Criteria: demonstrated unreliability, conflicts of interest, or outright falsification

### Rating Application
1. Identify the specific claim the evidence supports
2. Assess the source against the criteria for each grade
3. Assign the grade and document the reasoning
4. Flag any factors that could upgrade or downgrade the rating
5. Note the grade alongside the evidence in all downstream documents

## Outputs
- A grade (A through F) for each piece of evidence
- Written justification for each rating
- An evidence inventory sorted by grade for easy reference
- Clear communication of evidence quality to downstream consumers

## Common Pitfalls
- **Grade inflation**: Rating evidence higher than warranted because it supports a preferred conclusion. Apply criteria strictly.
- **Confusing source prestige with evidence quality**: A famous institution can produce weak evidence. Rate the specific evidence, not the brand.
- **Ignoring context**: Grade C evidence may be the best available in an emerging field. Note the ceiling, do not penalize unfairly.
- **Static ratings**: New information can upgrade or downgrade evidence. Revisit ratings as the research evolves.
- **Skipping the justification**: A grade without reasoning is useless. Always document why.
- **Binary thinking**: Avoid treating everything as either trustworthy or not. The gradient exists for a reason.
