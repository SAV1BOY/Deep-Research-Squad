# Confidence Map

## Purpose
A visual and structured map of confidence levels across all research findings. Provides decision-makers with a clear view of where the research stands on firm ground, where uncertainty remains, and where findings should be treated with caution. Prevents the false impression that all findings carry equal weight.

## When to Use
- When presenting research findings to decision-makers
- After completing evidence gathering and validation
- When the research covers multiple sub-topics with varying evidence strength
- When decisions must be made despite incomplete information

## Inputs
- All research findings organized by topic or question
- Evidence ratings for supporting evidence (from the Evidence Rating framework)
- Contradiction resolution status (from the Contradiction Map)
- Source reliability assessments for key sources
- The researcher's own assessment of completeness and certainty

## Process

### 1. List All Findings
Enumerate every finding or conclusion the research has produced. Group them by theme or research question for clarity.

### 2. Assign Confidence Levels
For each finding, assign one of three levels:

**High Confidence**
- Supported by Grade A or B evidence from multiple independent sources
- No unresolved contradictions
- Consistent across methodologies, time periods, and contexts
- The researcher would be surprised if this finding were overturned
- Label: "High" with green indicator

**Medium Confidence**
- Supported by Grade B or C evidence, possibly from limited sources
- Minor contradictions that have been reasonably resolved
- Plausible and consistent with broader knowledge but not fully confirmed
- The researcher considers this the most likely interpretation but acknowledges alternatives
- Label: "Medium" with yellow indicator

**Low Confidence**
- Supported only by Grade C, D, or E evidence
- Significant unresolved contradictions or data gaps
- Based on limited sources, extrapolation, or inference
- The researcher considers this a working hypothesis, not a conclusion
- Label: "Low" with red indicator

### 3. Justify Each Assignment
For every finding, write a one to three sentence justification explaining why it received its confidence level. Reference specific evidence grades, source counts, and contradiction status.

### 4. Identify Confidence Gaps
Highlight areas where confidence could be improved with additional research:
- What specific evidence would upgrade a finding from Medium to High?
- What unresolved contradictions are keeping confidence at Low?
- Is additional research feasible within project constraints?

### 5. Construct the Map
Organize findings visually:
- Group by research question or theme
- Use the three-level color coding consistently
- Place the most critical findings (those informing key decisions) prominently
- Include justification summaries alongside each finding

## Outputs
- A confidence map document showing all findings with their confidence levels
- Justification for each confidence assignment
- A gap analysis identifying what would increase confidence
- A summary view for executive audiences highlighting the overall confidence profile

## Common Pitfalls
- **Uniform confidence**: Rating everything as Medium is uninformative. Force differentiation. Aim for distribution across all three levels.
- **Overconfidence**: Assigning High confidence without sufficient evidence diversity. High confidence requires multiple independent, high-quality sources.
- **Underconfidence as hedging**: Rating everything Low to avoid accountability helps no one. Be honest about what the evidence actually supports.
- **Missing justifications**: A confidence level without explanation is an opinion, not an assessment. Always justify.
- **Ignoring the map in decisions**: Building the map but then making recommendations that ignore low-confidence findings is inconsistent. Align recommendations with confidence levels.
- **Static maps**: As new evidence arrives, confidence levels should be updated. The map is a living document throughout the research lifecycle.
