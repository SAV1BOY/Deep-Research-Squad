# Contradiction Map

## Purpose
A systematic framework for identifying, classifying, and resolving contradictions between sources or findings. Contradictions are inevitable in research; this framework ensures they are surfaced explicitly rather than buried or ignored, and that resolution approaches are principled.

## When to Use
- When two or more sources make conflicting claims about the same topic
- When data from different time periods or geographies tells different stories
- When expert opinions diverge on key questions
- During synthesis, when reconciling findings into a coherent narrative

## Inputs
- Two or more findings or claims that appear to conflict
- The sources behind each conflicting claim, with their evidence ratings
- Context about the conditions under which each claim was made
- Domain knowledge about what kinds of contradictions are expected

## Process

### 1. Identify Contradictions
Surface all instances where findings conflict:
- Direct contradiction: Source A says X, Source B says not-X
- Magnitude disagreement: Both agree on direction but differ significantly on scale
- Temporal contradiction: Claim was true at time T1 but not at T2
- Scope contradiction: Claim holds for population A but not population B
- Methodological contradiction: Different methods yield different conclusions

### 2. Classify Type
Assign each contradiction a type:
- **Factual**: Disagreement on verifiable facts (dates, numbers, events)
- **Interpretive**: Same facts but different conclusions drawn
- **Definitional**: Different definitions of key terms leading to apparent conflict
- **Contextual**: True in different contexts, not actually contradictory when context is specified
- **Methodological**: Different research methods producing different results

### 3. Assess Severity
Rate the impact of each contradiction on the research:
- **Critical**: Contradiction affects the core research question or primary recommendation
- **Significant**: Affects important secondary findings or changes the weight of evidence
- **Minor**: Affects peripheral details that do not change the overall picture
- **Cosmetic**: Differences in framing or emphasis with no substantive impact

### 4. Determine Resolution Approach
For each contradiction, apply the appropriate resolution strategy:
- **Evidence weighting**: Favor the claim supported by higher-grade evidence
- **Source reliability**: Favor the more reliable source per the Source Reliability framework
- **Recency**: Favor more recent evidence when the topic is time-sensitive
- **Specificity**: Favor the more specific and contextualized claim
- **Synthesis**: Both claims may be partially true; construct a more nuanced position
- **Escalate**: When resolution is not possible, flag the contradiction explicitly in outputs

### 5. Document Resolution
For each contradiction, record:
- The conflicting claims and their sources
- The type and severity classification
- The resolution approach applied
- The resolved position or the explicit acknowledgment of unresolved tension

## Outputs
- A contradiction inventory listing all identified conflicts
- Classification of each by type and severity
- Resolution status and approach for each contradiction
- Unresolved contradictions flagged for decision-maker awareness

## Common Pitfalls
- **Ignoring contradictions**: The most dangerous pitfall. Contradictions that are buried undermine the entire research.
- **Premature resolution**: Resolving contradictions too quickly without sufficient investigation. Sit with tension before resolving.
- **Always favoring the majority**: Three weak sources do not outweigh one strong source. Quality beats quantity.
- **False equivalence**: Presenting contradictions as "both sides" when evidence strongly favors one position.
- **Treating all contradictions as factual**: Many contradictions dissolve when you realize they are definitional or contextual.
- **Resolution bias**: Unconsciously resolving contradictions in favor of the preferred narrative. Apply criteria mechanically.
