# Evidence Weight Calibration

## Purpose
A structured approach for assigning and adjusting the relative weight of individual pieces of evidence when forming research conclusions. Ensures that stronger evidence exerts proportionally more influence on findings than weaker evidence, and that the weighting rationale is transparent and defensible.

## When to Apply
- When multiple pieces of evidence point in different directions and a conclusion must be reached
- When evidence varies widely in quality, recency, relevance, or source reliability
- When stakeholders need to understand why certain evidence was prioritized over other evidence
- When a research finding rests on a small number of high-weight evidence items and this concentration risk should be explicit

## Core Principles
1. **Not all evidence is equal**: A controlled study and an anonymous blog post both constitute evidence, but they should not carry the same weight in forming conclusions.
2. **Weight reflects multiple dimensions**: Evidence weight is a function of source reliability, methodological rigor, relevance to the specific question, recency, and independence from other evidence.
3. **Weighting must be explicit**: Implicit weighting (giving more attention to evidence that supports preferred conclusions) is the default human behavior. Explicit weighting counteracts it.
4. **Calibrate, do not calculate**: Evidence weighting is a structured judgment exercise, not a mathematical formula. The goal is defensible reasoning, not false precision.
5. **Reassess as evidence accumulates**: Weights assigned early in research may need revision as new evidence changes the picture.

## Step-by-Step Methodology

### Step 1: Inventory All Evidence
List every piece of evidence relevant to the conclusion being formed. Key question: Have we included all evidence, including evidence that contradicts our emerging conclusion?

### Step 2: Assess Source Reliability
For each piece of evidence, evaluate the credibility and track record of its source. Key question: Does this source have expertise, accountability, and a history of accuracy on this type of claim?

### Step 3: Evaluate Methodological Rigor
Assess how the evidence was produced: controlled experiment, observational study, expert opinion, anecdote, model output. Key question: How confident can we be that this evidence accurately represents reality, given how it was generated?

### Step 4: Judge Relevance to the Specific Question
Determine how directly each piece of evidence speaks to the exact question under investigation. Key question: Does this evidence address our specific question, or is it about a related but different question that we are extrapolating from?

### Step 5: Assess Recency and Currency
Evaluate whether the evidence reflects current conditions or a historical state that may no longer hold. Key question: Have conditions changed materially since this evidence was produced?

### Step 6: Check Independence
Determine whether pieces of evidence are truly independent or whether they derive from the same original source, dataset, or analysis. Key question: If the original source is wrong, how many of our evidence items fall simultaneously?

### Step 7: Assign Weight Categories
Place each piece of evidence into a weight category: anchor (forms the foundation of the conclusion), supporting (reinforces but does not independently establish), contextual (provides background but weak direct support), or marginal (noted but given minimal influence). Key question: If this single piece of evidence were removed, would the conclusion change?

### Step 8: Stress-Test the Weighting
Examine whether the conclusion survives if the highest-weighted evidence is removed or discredited. Key question: Is our conclusion robust to the loss of any single evidence item, or does it depend entirely on one or two anchor items?

## Output Format
- Evidence inventory table with columns for source, type, reliability rating, relevance rating, recency, independence flag, and assigned weight category
- Weight distribution summary showing how many anchor, supporting, contextual, and marginal items support the conclusion
- Concentration risk statement identifying any conclusion that depends on fewer than three independent anchor-weight items
- Sensitivity note describing how the conclusion would change if the highest-weighted items were discredited

## Integration with Other Frameworks
- **Evidence Rating**: Evidence rating provides the quality inputs that inform weight assignment.
- **Source Reliability**: Source reliability scores feed directly into the source reliability dimension of weight calibration.
- **Confidence Weighting**: The weight distribution determines the confidence level of the overall finding.
- **Bayesian Updating**: As new evidence arrives, weights are recalibrated and posterior probabilities updated.
- **Contradiction Map**: Contradictions between high-weight evidence items require explicit resolution.

## Anti-Patterns
- **Uniform weighting**: Treating all evidence as equally important, which lets low-quality evidence dilute high-quality evidence.
- **Confirmation weighting**: Unconsciously assigning higher weight to evidence that supports the preferred conclusion.
- **Authority-only weighting**: Weighting solely on source prestige without assessing methodological rigor or relevance.
- **Recency bias weighting**: Automatically weighting newer evidence higher without assessing whether the newer evidence is actually better.
- **Weight-and-forget**: Assigning weights once and never revisiting them as new evidence emerges or the research question sharpens.
- **Emotional weighting**: Giving disproportionate weight to vivid, narrative-rich evidence (case studies, anecdotes) over dry but methodologically superior evidence (large-sample studies, meta-analyses).
