# Goodhart's Law and Campbell's Law for Research

## Purpose
Apply Goodhart's Law ("When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure") and Campbell's Law ("The more a quantitative indicator is used for decision-making, the more it will be subject to corruption pressures") to research evaluation. These laws explain why metrics, rankings, and indicators often mislead when relied upon uncritically.

## When to Use
- When research relies on quantitative metrics or indicators as evidence
- When evaluating the validity of performance metrics, rankings, or indices
- When assessing whether incentive structures are distorting the data you are analyzing
- When metrics-driven targets appear to be met but real-world outcomes have not improved
- When designing measurement frameworks for research outputs

## Inputs
- Metrics, indicators, or rankings used as evidence in the research
- Knowledge of how the metrics are collected and who controls the data
- Understanding of the incentive structures surrounding the metrics
- Historical data showing how the metric has behaved after becoming a target

## Process

### 1. Identify the Metrics in Play
Catalog all quantitative indicators used in the research:
- What metrics are being used as proxies for underlying phenomena?
- Who collects and reports these metrics?
- What decisions or evaluations depend on these metrics?
- How long have these metrics been used as targets or benchmarks?

### 2. Assess Gaming Risk
For each metric, evaluate the risk of Goodhart distortion:
- **High gaming risk**: The metric is a target linked to rewards or punishments. Those being measured have the ability to influence the metric. The metric is a proxy for something harder to measure.
- **Moderate gaming risk**: The metric is tracked and reported but not directly tied to consequences. Some ability to influence exists.
- **Low gaming risk**: The metric is observed passively, those being measured are unaware of it, or it directly measures the outcome of interest.

### 3. Look for Distortion Patterns
Check whether the metric is being gamed:
- **Teaching to the test**: Optimizing specifically for the metric while neglecting the underlying goal
- **Cream-skimming**: Selecting favorable cases to improve metrics while excluding difficult ones
- **Threshold effects**: Clustering of results just above a target threshold, suggesting manipulation
- **Divergence**: The metric improves while real-world outcomes stagnate or decline
- **Redefinition**: Changing how the metric is calculated to produce better numbers without changing reality

### 4. Validate with Triangulation
When a metric is at risk of distortion:
- Cross-check the metric against independent indicators of the same underlying phenomenon
- Use qualitative evidence to verify that quantitative improvements are real
- Compare self-reported metrics with externally audited or independently collected data
- Look for the metric's behavior before and after it became a target

### 5. Design Metric-Resistant Research
Build research that is robust to Goodhart effects:
- Use multiple metrics rather than a single indicator
- Include metrics that are harder to game (lagging indicators, independently measured outcomes)
- Combine quantitative metrics with qualitative assessment
- Rotate or update metrics periodically to prevent optimization against a fixed target
- Measure outcomes directly where possible rather than relying on proxies

### 6. Report Metric Limitations
In research outputs:
- Disclose when key findings depend on metrics that may be subject to gaming
- Note the incentive structures around the metrics used
- Distinguish between metrics that directly measure outcomes and those that are proxies
- Recommend additional verification when findings rest on potentially distorted metrics

## Outputs
- Assessment of gaming risk for all key metrics used in the research
- Identification of specific distortion patterns where found
- Triangulation results validating or questioning metric-based findings
- Recommendations for metric-robust research design

## Common Pitfalls
- **Naive trust in numbers**: Assuming that because something is quantified, it is accurate. Numbers can lie as effectively as words.
- **Dismissing all metrics**: The opposite error. Metrics are valuable when understood and used carefully. The goal is critical use, not rejection.
- **Ignoring incentives**: Failing to ask "Who benefits from this metric looking good?" is the most common oversight.
- **Assuming gaming is deliberate**: Sometimes Goodhart effects emerge without conscious manipulation. System incentives shape behavior unconsciously.
- **Overlooking Campbell's corruption pressure**: The more important a metric becomes, the more pressure exists to distort it. High-stakes metrics are the most suspect.
- **Single-metric fixation**: Relying on one metric for a complex phenomenon almost guarantees a Goodhart problem. Always use multiple indicators.
