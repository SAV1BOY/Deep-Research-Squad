# Ioannidis: Why Most Published Research Findings Are False

## Purpose
Apply John Ioannidis' landmark analysis of why most published research findings are false to evidence evaluation. Ioannidis demonstrated mathematically that the majority of published findings in many fields are likely to be wrong due to structural features of the research process. This framework helps researchers account for these structural problems when assessing evidence.

## When to Use
- When evaluating published research studies as evidence
- When a research finding seems surprising or too good to be true
- When assessing the reliability of a body of literature in a field
- When determining how much weight to give a published study
- When designing research to avoid common validity threats

## Inputs
- Published research findings to evaluate
- Information about the study's methodology, sample size, and effect size
- Knowledge of the field's research practices and incentive structures
- Information about replication attempts, if any
- Understanding of publication processes and biases in the field

## Process

### 1. Assess Prior Probability
The likelihood that a finding is true depends on the prior probability that the hypothesis was correct:
- In a field with many untested hypotheses (genomics, early-stage drug discovery), most hypotheses are false a priori, so most "significant" findings will be false positives
- In a field with well-grounded hypotheses, the prior probability is higher and findings are more trustworthy
- Ask: how plausible was this hypothesis before the study was conducted?
- Low prior probability combined with marginal statistical significance strongly suggests a false positive

### 2. Evaluate Statistical Power
Underpowered studies are more likely to produce false findings:
- What was the sample size relative to the expected effect size?
- Was a power analysis conducted before the study?
- Small studies detecting large effects are suspicious: the effect is probably inflated
- When many underpowered studies investigate the same question, the published ones are likely to overestimate the effect

### 3. Check for Flexibility in Analysis
Greater flexibility increases the probability of false findings:
- How many outcomes were measured? Were they pre-specified?
- How many statistical tests were run? Was correction for multiple comparisons applied?
- Were subgroup analyses pre-planned or post-hoc?
- Could the researchers have analyzed the data differently and gotten a different result?
- The more analytical degrees of freedom, the less trustworthy the finding

### 4. Evaluate Publication Bias
The published literature is a biased sample of all research conducted:
- Positive results are published more often than negative results
- The published literature therefore overestimates effect sizes and the proportion of true findings
- Look for signs of publication bias: are there only positive findings? Is there a funnel plot asymmetry?
- Check for unpublished replications or contradictory studies in preprint servers or grey literature

### 5. Assess Field-Level Factors
Some fields are structurally more prone to false findings:
- **Hot fields** with many competing teams produce rushed, less rigorous work
- **Fields with small effects** need large samples that are rarely obtained
- **Fields with financial interests** face incentive pressures that bias results
- **Fields with weak methodology standards** accumulate more false findings
- Consider the field-level context when evaluating any single finding

### 6. Look for Replication
The strongest evidence for a finding is successful independent replication:
- Has the finding been replicated by independent researchers using different samples?
- Were replication attempts adequately powered?
- Did the effect size hold up in replication, or did it shrink significantly?
- A finding that has survived multiple rigorous replication attempts is much more trustworthy
- A finding that has never been replicated should be treated as preliminary

### 7. Apply the Ioannidis Discount
When incorporating published findings into research:
- Discount findings based on the factors above
- A single unreplicated study with flexible methods in a hot field deserves little weight
- A multiply replicated finding with pre-registered methods deserves substantial weight
- Make the discount explicit in your evidence rating and confidence map

## Outputs
- Assessment of each key finding's vulnerability to Ioannidis factors
- Adjusted confidence levels accounting for structural research problems
- Identification of findings that require replication before reliance
- Recommendations for which published evidence to weight heavily and which to discount

## Common Pitfalls
- **Nihilism**: Concluding that no research can be trusted. Ioannidis identifies problems to fix, not reasons to abandon evidence-based reasoning.
- **Ignoring the framework for convenient findings**: Applying the discount only to findings you disagree with. Apply it uniformly.
- **Equating published with true**: The default assumption should not be that a published finding is correct. It should be assessed.
- **Ignoring effect sizes**: Statistical significance without practical significance is common. Focus on whether the effect matters, not just whether p < 0.05.
- **Overlooking pre-registration**: Pre-registered studies with pre-specified analyses are much more trustworthy. Check for pre-registration.
- **Assuming replication failure means fraud**: Most false findings result from structural problems (low power, flexibility, publication bias), not dishonesty.
