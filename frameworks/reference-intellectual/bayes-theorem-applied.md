# Bayes' Theorem Applied

## Purpose
Apply Bayesian reasoning to research: systematically update beliefs as new evidence arrives. Instead of treating each piece of evidence in isolation, Bayesian thinking maintains a running estimate that integrates prior knowledge with new data. This framework operationalizes the core equation: posterior belief equals prior belief updated by the likelihood of the evidence.

## When to Use
- When research involves accumulating evidence over time
- When you need to combine prior knowledge with new findings
- When assessing how much a specific piece of evidence should change your confidence
- When evaluating competing hypotheses with incoming data
- When calibrating confidence levels for the Confidence Map

## Inputs
- A prior belief or estimate (your best assessment before new evidence)
- New evidence to be incorporated
- An assessment of the evidence's diagnostic value (likelihood ratio)
- Alternative hypotheses that the evidence might support

## Process

### 1. Establish the Prior
Before encountering new evidence, state your current belief:
- What does existing knowledge suggest about the question?
- Use base rates where available: how often does this type of thing occur generally?
- Express the prior as a probability or probability range
- Be honest about the prior's basis: is it grounded in data, expert consensus, or rough intuition?
- A well-calibrated prior is important but not sacred; it will be updated

### 2. Assess the Evidence's Diagnostic Value
Not all evidence is equally informative. Evaluate the likelihood ratio:
- If the hypothesis is true, how likely would you be to see this evidence?
- If the hypothesis is false, how likely would you be to see this evidence?
- The ratio of these two likelihoods determines how much the evidence should move your belief
- Evidence that is equally likely under both hypotheses is not diagnostic and should not move your estimate
- Evidence that is much more likely under one hypothesis than another is highly diagnostic

### 3. Update the Belief
Combine the prior with the evidence:
- Strong diagnostic evidence plus a weak prior can produce a moderate posterior
- Weak diagnostic evidence plus a strong prior leaves the posterior close to the prior
- Multiple pieces of independent evidence compound their effects
- Update incrementally: each piece of evidence nudges the belief, not replaces it
- Track the direction and magnitude of each update

### 4. Consider Alternative Hypotheses
Bayesian updating works best when considering multiple hypotheses:
- For each piece of evidence, ask: which hypothesis does this evidence favor most?
- Do not just test one hypothesis; compare the evidence's fit across all plausible explanations
- An alternative hypothesis that explains the evidence better should gain probability at the expense of others
- Regularly check whether a hypothesis you have not considered might explain the evidence pattern

### 5. Iterate
Bayesian reasoning is continuous:
- After each update, the posterior becomes the new prior for the next piece of evidence
- Maintain an update log showing how beliefs have evolved
- Check for consistency: has the accumulation of evidence moved beliefs in a sensible direction?
- Be alert for evidence that should produce large updates (paradigm-challenging findings)

### 6. Communicate Bayesian Reasoning
In research outputs:
- State the prior, the key evidence, and the posterior
- Explain why specific evidence was or was not diagnostic
- Show how the conclusion would change under different priors (sensitivity to assumptions)
- Use probability language consistently with the Superforecasting framework

## Outputs
- A documented chain from prior through evidence to posterior belief
- An update log showing how beliefs evolved through the research process
- Sensitivity analysis showing how different priors would change the conclusion
- Clear probability estimates for key research questions

## Common Pitfalls
- **Ignoring the prior**: Treating each piece of evidence as if no prior knowledge exists leads to overreaction to individual findings.
- **Anchoring on the prior**: Refusing to update despite strong diagnostic evidence. The prior is a starting point, not a conclusion.
- **Neglecting base rates**: Individual case details are vivid but base rates are often more informative. Start with base rates.
- **Treating non-diagnostic evidence as diagnostic**: Evidence that is equally consistent with multiple hypotheses should not change your beliefs. Assess the likelihood ratio honestly.
- **Ignoring alternative hypotheses**: Confirmation of one hypothesis is only meaningful relative to how well alternatives explain the same evidence.
- **False precision**: Bayesian reasoning with made-up numbers is worse than qualitative reasoning. Use numbers only when you have a reasonable basis for them.
