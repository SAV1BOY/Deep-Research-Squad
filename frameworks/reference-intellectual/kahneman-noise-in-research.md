# Kahneman: Noise in Research

## Purpose
Apply Daniel Kahneman's concept of noise (unwanted variability in judgments) to research processes. While bias is directional error, noise is random scatter. Research judgments are affected by both, but noise is often invisible and unmanaged. This framework helps identify and reduce noise in research.

## When to Use
- When multiple researchers or agents are making judgments about the same evidence
- When consistency of evaluation matters (evidence rating, source assessment, confidence levels)
- When research outputs will inform high-stakes decisions
- When you suspect that different researchers would reach different conclusions from the same data

## Inputs
- Research judgments that require consistency (ratings, assessments, classifications)
- Multiple evaluators or evaluation instances
- Structured criteria from other frameworks (Evidence Rating, Source Reliability)
- Historical examples of judgment variability in similar contexts

## Process

### 1. Understand the Types of Noise
- **Level noise**: Different researchers have different baseline standards (one rates everything higher than another)
- **Pattern noise**: Different researchers respond differently to specific cases (one is lenient on methodology gaps, another is strict)
- **Occasion noise**: The same researcher gives different judgments at different times due to mood, fatigue, or order effects

### 2. Measure Noise
Before you can reduce noise, detect it:
- Have multiple researchers independently evaluate the same evidence
- Compare their ratings, classifications, and conclusions
- Calculate the spread: how much do judgments vary for the same input?
- Distinguish between legitimate disagreement (different information) and noise (same information, different conclusions)

### 3. Apply Noise Reduction Strategies
- **Structured judgment**: Use frameworks with explicit criteria rather than holistic impressions. The Evidence Rating framework is a noise reduction tool.
- **Independent evaluation first**: Each researcher forms their judgment before seeing others' judgments. Then aggregate.
- **Calibration exercises**: Periodically evaluate reference cases with known answers to align standards.
- **Decision hygiene**: Sequence information carefully; avoid anchoring on early data. Present the most diagnostic information first.
- **Decomposition**: Break complex judgments into simpler components. Judge each component separately, then combine.

### 4. Distinguish Noise from Signal
Not all variability is noise:
- Legitimate disagreement based on different expertise or perspective is valuable
- Challenge assumptions about what constitutes the "correct" judgment
- When two competent researchers disagree, investigate whether one has information the other lacks

### 5. Build Noise Audits into Process
- Periodically check consistency by having independent evaluations of the same evidence
- Track whether noise levels are changing over time
- Review whether noise is higher in certain types of judgments (complexity correlates with noise)

## Outputs
- Awareness of noise sources in the research process
- Structured judgment protocols that reduce unwanted variability
- Calibration mechanisms for maintaining consistency
- Noise audit results showing where variability exists

## Common Pitfalls
- **Ignoring noise entirely**: Most research processes focus on bias and ignore noise. Both matter equally.
- **Eliminating all variability**: Some disagreement is productive. Do not enforce false consensus.
- **Over-structuring**: Excessive rules can reduce noise but also reduce the ability to detect genuinely unusual cases. Balance structure with judgment.
- **Confusing noise with expertise differences**: A specialist and a generalist may legitimately disagree. That is not noise.
- **Measuring noise without acting on it**: Detecting variability and then ignoring it wastes the diagnostic effort.
- **Anchoring on the first judgment**: When aggregating multiple evaluations, the first one shared often anchors the group. Use independent evaluation first.
