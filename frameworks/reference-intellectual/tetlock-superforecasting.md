# Tetlock: Superforecasting

## Purpose
Apply Philip Tetlock's superforecasting principles to research predictions and assessments. Superforecasters consistently outperform experts by using specific cognitive strategies. This framework distills those strategies for application to research judgments, especially when estimating probabilities, trends, or outcomes.

## When to Use
- When research requires making predictions or probability estimates
- When assessing the likelihood of future events or trends
- When calibrating confidence levels for research findings
- When evaluating forecasts made by others (analysts, experts, models)

## Inputs
- Questions that require probabilistic judgment
- Base rate data for similar situations
- Current evidence and its quality ratings
- Prior forecasts or estimates to update
- Feedback on past forecasting accuracy where available

## Process

### 1. Calibration
Align stated confidence with actual accuracy:
- When you say 70% confident, you should be right about 70% of the time
- Most people are overconfident: their 90% estimates are right only 70% of the time
- Practice calibration by tracking predictions and outcomes over time
- Use reference classes: how often does this type of thing actually happen?

### 2. Granularity
Use precise probabilities rather than vague language:
- Replace "likely" with "75% probability" and "unlikely" with "15% probability"
- Finer-grained estimates force more careful thinking
- Distinguish between 60% and 80% confidence; they imply very different evidence bases
- Avoid the trap of false precision: 73% is not meaningfully different from 72%

### 3. Updating
Revise estimates as new evidence arrives:
- Start with a prior estimate based on base rates and existing knowledge
- Adjust incrementally with each new piece of evidence
- Update in proportion to the evidence's diagnostic value, not its vividness
- Avoid under-updating (anchoring on the prior) and over-updating (overreacting to new data)
- Track the direction and magnitude of updates to maintain an audit trail

### 4. Foxes vs Hedgehogs
Think like a fox, not a hedgehog:
- Hedgehogs know one big thing and force all evidence into their framework
- Foxes know many things and draw from multiple models and perspectives
- Integrate insights from different domains, methods, and viewpoints
- Be willing to say "it depends" and specify what it depends on
- Resist the appeal of grand narratives that explain everything

### 5. Decomposition
Break complex questions into simpler, more estimable components:
- Instead of "Will X happen?" ask "What needs to be true for X to happen?" and estimate each component
- Combine component estimates using simple probability rules
- This reduces the cognitive load of each individual judgment
- Identify which components you are most and least certain about

### 6. Postmortem Discipline
Learn from outcomes:
- When a prediction is resolved, compare the outcome to your estimate
- Was the outcome within the range you expected? If not, why?
- Identify systematic errors in your forecasting process
- Distinguish between being wrong due to bad reasoning and being wrong due to irreducible uncertainty

## Outputs
- Calibrated probability estimates for key research questions
- An updating log showing how estimates changed with new evidence
- Decomposed forecasts showing which components drive the overall estimate
- A forecasting track record for ongoing calibration improvement

## Common Pitfalls
- **Overconfidence**: The most common forecasting error. Systematically widen your uncertainty ranges.
- **Anchoring on the first estimate**: Initial estimates exert excessive pull on final judgments. Actively consider how wrong the anchor might be.
- **Narrative seduction**: A compelling story is not a good forecast. Demand data and base rates.
- **Ignoring base rates**: Individual case details are vivid but base rates are more informative. Always start with "how often does this generally happen?"
- **Binary thinking**: Treating outcomes as certain or impossible when they are probabilistic. The world is rarely 0% or 100%.
- **Not tracking accuracy**: Without feedback, forecasting skill cannot improve. Record predictions and check them against outcomes.
