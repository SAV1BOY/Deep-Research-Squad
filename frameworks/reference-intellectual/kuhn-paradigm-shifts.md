# Kuhn: Paradigm Shifts

## Purpose
Apply Thomas Kuhn's theory of scientific revolutions to research analysis. Kuhn argued that science does not progress linearly but through cycles of normal science (working within an accepted paradigm) and revolutionary science (paradigm shifts). This framework helps researchers recognize when findings operate within existing paradigms and when they signal potential paradigm shifts.

## When to Use
- When research findings challenge widely accepted frameworks or assumptions
- When evaluating whether an emerging trend represents incremental change or fundamental disruption
- When assessing why experts disagree: they may be operating from different paradigms
- When historical analysis reveals shifts in understanding that reframe current debates

## Inputs
- Research findings, especially those that challenge conventional wisdom
- The current dominant paradigm or framework in the research domain
- Historical examples of paradigm shifts in the relevant field
- Anomalies: findings that do not fit the existing paradigm

## Process

### 1. Identify the Current Paradigm
Map the dominant framework that shapes understanding in the domain:
- What are the foundational assumptions that most experts share?
- What methods and approaches are considered standard?
- What questions are considered important and which are dismissed?
- What constitutes a valid explanation within this paradigm?
- A paradigm is often invisible to those inside it: articulating it requires deliberate effort

### 2. Detect Anomalies
Identify findings that do not fit the paradigm:
- What results are consistently unexpected or unexplained?
- What problems are considered "unsolvable" within the current framework?
- What findings do practitioners dismiss as noise, outliers, or measurement error?
- Are anomalies accumulating over time?
- Kuhn's key insight: anomalies are initially ignored or explained away, but they accumulate

### 3. Assess Paradigm Health
Determine whether the paradigm is in normal science or pre-revolutionary crisis:
- **Normal science**: The paradigm explains most observations well. Anomalies are few and manageable. Puzzle-solving within the framework is productive.
- **Crisis**: Anomalies are numerous, persistent, and central. Ad hoc modifications proliferate. Competing frameworks begin to emerge. Confidence in the paradigm is declining.
- **Revolution**: A new paradigm is proposed that explains anomalies better. Converts are shifting allegiance. The old guard resists.

### 4. Evaluate Competing Paradigms
When multiple frameworks compete:
- Each paradigm is partly incommensurable: they define terms differently and value different evidence
- Avoid judging a new paradigm solely by the old paradigm's standards
- Assess each paradigm by its ability to explain anomalies, generate predictions, and inspire productive research
- Recognize that paradigm choice involves judgment, not just evidence

### 5. Apply to Research Practice
Use paradigm awareness to improve research quality:
- When operating within a paradigm: be productive but stay alert for anomalies
- When encountering anomalies: investigate rather than dismiss
- When paradigms compete: present findings in terms both paradigms can engage with
- When a shift is underway: document the transition and its implications for existing knowledge

### 6. Communicate Paradigm Context
In research outputs:
- State which paradigm frames the analysis
- Note findings that are paradigm-dependent (would be interpreted differently under a different framework)
- Flag emerging paradigm challenges and their potential significance
- Help decision-makers understand that paradigm shifts change what counts as relevant

## Outputs
- Identification of the dominant paradigm in the research domain
- Anomaly inventory with assessment of their significance
- Assessment of paradigm health (normal, crisis, or revolutionary)
- Implications of potential paradigm shift for the research conclusions

## Common Pitfalls
- **Crying revolution**: Not every anomaly signals a paradigm shift. Most anomalies are resolved within the existing framework. Be cautious about declaring revolutions.
- **Paradigm blindness**: Being so embedded in the current paradigm that anomalies are invisible. Actively seek disconfirming evidence.
- **False equivalence between paradigms**: Competing paradigms are not always equally valid. Evidence and explanatory power still matter.
- **Nostalgia for the old paradigm**: Resistance to new frameworks because the old one is familiar. Evaluate frameworks on merit.
- **Over-applying the concept**: Kuhn described scientific revolutions. Not every change in business, technology, or policy is a paradigm shift.
- **Ignoring the sociology**: Paradigm shifts are social as well as intellectual. Power, prestige, and institutional inertia affect the transition.
