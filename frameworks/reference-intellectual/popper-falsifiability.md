# Popper: Falsifiability

## Purpose
Apply Karl Popper's criterion of falsifiability to research claims and hypotheses. A claim is scientific (and therefore testable) only if it can, in principle, be proven false. This framework helps researchers distinguish testable hypotheses from unfalsifiable assertions, strengthening the rigor of research conclusions.

## When to Use
- When formulating research hypotheses before investigation
- When evaluating claims from sources: are they testable or merely assertive?
- When assessing the rigor of arguments encountered during research
- When a finding seems compelling but may be unfalsifiable (and therefore untestable)

## Inputs
- Research hypotheses or claims to be evaluated
- The evidence cited in support of each claim
- The logical structure of the argument behind the claim
- Knowledge of what evidence would contradict the claim

## Process

### 1. State the Claim Precisely
Before testing falsifiability, ensure the claim is stated clearly:
- Remove ambiguity: vague claims are harder to falsify and therefore weaker
- Identify the specific prediction the claim makes
- Determine the scope: does the claim apply universally or conditionally?
- Rewrite the claim in a form that makes its predictions explicit

### 2. Identify the Falsifier
Ask: what evidence, if found, would prove this claim wrong?
- If you can identify specific, observable evidence that would contradict the claim, it is falsifiable
- If no conceivable evidence could disprove the claim, it is not falsifiable and should be treated as an assumption, belief, or definition rather than a finding
- The easier it is to specify the falsifier, the stronger the claim

### 3. Test Against the Evidence
Actively seek disconfirming evidence, not just confirming evidence:
- Confirmation is cheap: almost any claim can find some supporting evidence
- Disconfirmation is informative: a single clear counterexample can refute a universal claim
- Design searches specifically to find evidence against the hypothesis
- Apply the Search Architecture framework with explicit disconfirmation queries

### 4. Assess Degree of Corroboration
A hypothesis that has survived serious attempts at falsification is corroborated (not proven):
- How many independent tests has the claim survived?
- How severe were those tests? (A test that could easily have falsified the claim but did not is more informative)
- Has the claim been tested across different contexts, time periods, and methods?
- Corroboration is a matter of degree, not a binary state

### 5. Handle Unfalsifiable Claims
When a claim cannot be falsified:
- Label it explicitly as an assumption, axiom, or value judgment
- Do not present it as a research finding
- Assess whether the broader argument depends on this unfalsifiable element
- Note it in the research output for transparency

### 6. Guard Against Ad Hoc Rescue
Watch for modifications that save a claim from falsification:
- When disconfirming evidence appears, does the source add exceptions or qualifications to preserve the claim?
- A claim that is continuously modified to avoid falsification loses credibility
- Each modification should be independently testable; otherwise it is ad hoc

## Outputs
- Classification of each research claim as falsifiable or unfalsifiable
- Identified falsifiers for each testable claim
- Assessment of corroboration level based on surviving falsification attempts
- Flagged claims that rely on unfalsifiable assumptions

## Common Pitfalls
- **Confirmation bias**: Searching only for evidence that supports the hypothesis. Actively seek disconfirmation.
- **Moving the goalposts**: Redefining the claim after disconfirming evidence appears. Lock the claim before testing.
- **Confusing unfalsifiable with true**: An unfalsifiable claim may be true, but it cannot be scientifically supported. Distinguish the categories.
- **Applying too strictly**: In social sciences and applied research, perfect falsifiability is rare. Assess degree of falsifiability rather than demanding a binary.
- **Ignoring probabilistic claims**: "X increases the probability of Y" is harder to falsify than "X causes Y" but is still testable with sufficient data.
- **Dismissing all non-falsifiable knowledge**: Values, definitions, and logical frameworks are unfalsifiable but essential. The framework applies to empirical claims.
