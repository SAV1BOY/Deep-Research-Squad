# Evidence Ladder Application Framework

## Purpose
Apply a structured evidence hierarchy (the "evidence ladder") to grade each piece of evidence collected during research. Maps different evidence types to strength ratings so that grading is consistent, transparent, and defensible across all claims and all agents.

## When to Use
- When populating the strength column in the Claim Grid.
- When comparing two pieces of evidence that support or contradict a claim.
- When deciding whether the evidence base is sufficient to support a conclusion.
- When advising the Synthesis Lead on how much weight to give different findings.

## Inputs
- A piece of evidence (data point, quote, finding, observation).
- The claim it is being used to support or refute.
- Metadata about the evidence source (type, methodology, provenance).

## Process

### Step 1: Identify the Evidence Type
Classify the evidence into one of the following types, listed from strongest to weakest:

**Rung 7 — Systematic Review or Meta-Analysis**
- Rigorous synthesis of all available evidence on a question.
- Follows a defined methodology for inclusion, exclusion, and analysis.
- Highest strength because it accounts for the full evidence landscape.

**Rung 6 — Controlled Experiment or Randomized Trial**
- Direct test of a hypothesis with control groups.
- Establishes causal relationships, not just correlations.

**Rung 5 — Large-Scale Observational Study**
- Statistical analysis of large datasets.
- Shows correlations and patterns but cannot confirm causation alone.

**Rung 4 — Expert Analysis with Methodology**
- Analysis by a recognized expert using a transparent, described methodology.
- Strength comes from both expertise and methodological rigor.

**Rung 3 — Case Study or Documented Example**
- Detailed examination of a specific instance.
- Provides depth but limited generalizability.

**Rung 2 — Expert Opinion without Methodology**
- Assertion by a recognized authority without transparent methodology.
- Carries weight due to expertise but is not independently verifiable.

**Rung 1 — Anecdotal or Self-Reported Evidence**
- Individual accounts, testimonials, or unverified claims.
- Lowest strength due to bias risk and lack of systematic collection.

### Step 2: Map Evidence Type to Strength Rating
| Rung | Evidence Type | Strength Rating |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | Systematic review / meta-analysis | Strong |
| 6 | Controlled experiment / RCT | Strong |
| 5 | Large-scale observational study | Moderate-Strong |
| 4 | Expert analysis with methodology | Moderate |
| 3 | Case study / documented example | Moderate-Weak |
| 2 | Expert opinion without methodology | Weak |
| 1 | Anecdotal / self-reported | Weak |

### Step 3: Apply Adjustments
The base rating from Step 2 can be adjusted up or down:

**Upgrade conditions:**
- Evidence has been independently replicated.
- The methodology has been peer-reviewed and validated.
- The sample size or data scope is exceptionally large.
- The evidence comes from a recognized gold-standard source.

**Downgrade conditions:**
- The methodology has known flaws or limitations.
- The sample is unrepresentative or too small.
- The evidence is from a potentially biased source.
- The evidence has been challenged by credible critique.
- The evidence is outdated and the field has evolved.

### Step 4: Document the Grade
- Record: Evidence description, rung classification, base rating, any adjustments, final rating.
- Include a one-sentence rationale for the final rating.
- This creates an audit trail for every strength assessment.

### Step 5: Assess Sufficiency for the Claim
- A single Rung 7 source may be sufficient for a high-confidence claim.
- Multiple Rung 3-4 sources may collectively support moderate confidence.
- Rung 1-2 sources alone are insufficient for any confident claim; flag for further investigation.
- When evidence at different rungs conflicts, higher-rung evidence generally prevails unless there are specific methodological concerns.

## Outputs
- A rung classification and strength rating for each piece of evidence.
- Adjustment notes explaining any upgrades or downgrades.
- Sufficiency assessment for each claim based on its evidence.
- Recommendations for additional evidence needed to strengthen weak claims.

## Common Pitfalls
- Applying the ladder rigidly without considering context. A well-designed case study may outweigh a flawed meta-analysis.
- Assuming higher rungs are always applicable. Some questions cannot be studied with experiments.
- Not adjusting for methodological quality within a rung.
- Treating the ladder as the only factor in evidence quality; independence and corroboration also matter.
- Ignoring that different fields have different gold standards (RCTs in medicine vs case studies in law).
- Using the ladder to dismiss valid evidence rather than to calibrate confidence.

## Related Frameworks
- **Claim Grid**: The ladder feeds directly into the strength column.
- **Cross-Source Verification**: Corroboration can upgrade evidence regardless of rung.
- **Citation Integrity**: Verify the evidence actually comes from the claimed methodology.
- **Falsification**: Even strong evidence should be tested against falsification attempts.
- **Primary-Secondary Split**: Primary sources often sit higher on the ladder than secondary ones.
