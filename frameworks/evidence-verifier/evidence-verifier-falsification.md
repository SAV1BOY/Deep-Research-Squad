# Falsification Framework

## Purpose
Mandate a deliberate attempt to disprove each key hypothesis or claim before accepting it as a research finding. Counteracts confirmation bias by requiring the researcher to actively seek evidence that would invalidate the conclusion, not just evidence that supports it.

## When to Use
- Before finalizing any high-confidence claim in the research output.
- When the evidence base appears unanimous (suspiciously so; real topics usually have dissent).
- When the research involves contested or politically charged topics.
- When the consequences of a wrong conclusion are significant.
- As a final quality check before the Synthesis Lead assembles the deliverable.

## Inputs
- A hypothesis or claim that the research currently supports.
- The evidence base supporting the claim (from the Claim Grid).
- The scope and domain of the research.

## Process

### Step 1: State the Hypothesis Clearly
- Write the hypothesis as a specific, testable statement.
- Remove hedge words. Instead of "X likely causes Y," write "X causes Y."
- The statement must be falsifiable: it must be possible to imagine evidence that would disprove it.
- If the hypothesis cannot be falsified even in principle, it is not a scientific claim; flag it as opinion or assumption.

### Step 2: Define What Would Disprove It
- For each hypothesis, explicitly state what evidence would disprove it.
- Be concrete: "If data shows X is below threshold T, the hypothesis is false."
- Identify at least three types of falsifying evidence:
  - **Direct counter-evidence**: Data or observations that directly contradict the claim.
  - **Methodological failure**: Flaws in the supporting evidence that undermine the conclusion.
  - **Alternative explanation**: A different hypothesis that explains the same evidence better.

### Step 3: Search for Falsifying Evidence
- Conduct targeted searches specifically designed to find counter-evidence.
- Search for: critiques of the claim, failed replications, contradictory data, dissenting expert opinions.
- Use query strategies biased toward finding disagreement:
  - "[claim] criticism"
  - "[claim] debunked"
  - "[claim] limitations"
  - "[claim] contrary evidence"
  - "[claim] failed replication"
- Do not limit to the same sources that supported the claim. Search different source classes.

### Step 4: Evaluate Any Falsifying Evidence Found
- If falsifying evidence is found, evaluate it with the same rigor as supporting evidence.
- Apply the Evidence Ladder to grade its strength.
- Check Citation Integrity.
- Determine: Does this evidence actually falsify the hypothesis, or does it address a different aspect?

### Step 5: Apply the Falsification Decision

**Hypothesis survives**: No credible falsifying evidence found despite genuine search effort.
- Confidence increases. Document the falsification attempt and its result.
- Note: Absence of falsifying evidence is not proof. It may simply be undiscovered.

**Hypothesis is weakened**: Some falsifying evidence found but it is not conclusive.
- Downgrade confidence. Add caveats to the claim.
- Document the falsifying evidence and explain why it weakens but does not disprove the hypothesis.

**Hypothesis is falsified**: Strong, credible evidence directly contradicts the hypothesis.
- Reject or substantially revise the hypothesis.
- Investigate whether the falsifying evidence supports an alternative hypothesis.
- Update the Claim Grid accordingly.

**Hypothesis is contested**: Strong evidence exists on both sides.
- Present both sides with evidence strength ratings.
- Do not force a resolution if the evidence genuinely supports both positions.
- Flag for the stakeholder as an unresolved debate.

### Step 6: Document the Falsification Attempt
- Record: Hypothesis, falsifying criteria defined, searches conducted, evidence found, decision, and rationale.
- This documentation is critical for research integrity and transparency.
- Even if no falsifying evidence is found, the attempt itself adds credibility.

### Step 7: Check for Confirmation Bias
- Review the full falsification process: Was the search genuinely rigorous?
- Would a skeptic find the falsification attempt convincing?
- Did the researcher unconsciously set a higher bar for falsifying evidence than for supporting evidence?
- If bias is suspected, have another agent review the falsification.

## Outputs
- A falsification log for each key hypothesis.
- Updated confidence levels post-falsification.
- Counter-evidence documentation with strength ratings.
- Revised hypotheses where falsification succeeded.
- Confirmation bias self-assessment.

## Common Pitfalls
- Performing a token falsification search without genuine effort.
- Setting the falsification bar impossibly high so the hypothesis is unfalsifiable in practice.
- Dismissing falsifying evidence on grounds that would not be applied to supporting evidence.
- Not searching diverse source classes for counter-evidence.
- Confusing "I did not find falsifying evidence" with "The hypothesis is proven."
- Skipping falsification for claims that "everyone knows" are true.
- Allowing confirmation bias to infect the evaluation of falsifying evidence.

## Related Frameworks
- **Claim Grid**: Falsification results update the confidence column.
- **Cross-Source Verification**: Disagreeing sources may constitute natural falsification.
- **Ladder Application**: Falsifying evidence is graded on the same ladder as supporting evidence.
- **Citation Integrity**: Falsifying evidence must pass the same integrity checks.
- **Problem Framing**: Questioning assumptions at the start is a form of preemptive falsification.
