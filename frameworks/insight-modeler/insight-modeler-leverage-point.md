# Leverage Point Identification Framework

## Purpose

Identify the most effective points for intervention in a complex system, based on Donella Meadows' hierarchy of leverage points. Move beyond surface-level parameter adjustments to find deeper structural, informational, and paradigmatic interventions that produce outsized effects relative to effort invested.

## When to Use

- When a system is not producing desired outcomes and intervention is needed.
- When resources for intervention are limited and must be focused for maximum impact.
- When past interventions have failed and a deeper analysis of the system is needed.
- When stakeholders are debating where to focus effort and need a structured framework for prioritization.

## Inputs

- A system map or causal model of the domain (from insight-modeler-causal-map.md or equivalent).
- Understanding of current system behavior and why it is unsatisfactory.
- Knowledge of past interventions and their effects.
- Desired system behavior or outcomes.

## Process

### Step 1: System Behavior Description
Describe the current behavior of the system. What does it do? What patterns does it exhibit? What outcomes does it produce? Contrast this with desired behavior.

### Step 2: Leverage Point Scan
Systematically scan the system at each level of Meadows' hierarchy, from least to most powerful:

**Level 12 - Constants and Parameters** (weakest leverage)
Numbers such as subsidies, taxes, standards. Easy to change but rarely alter system behavior fundamentally. Ask: are there parameter adjustments that could help?

**Level 11 - Buffer Sizes**
Stocks or inventories that stabilize the system. Larger buffers absorb shocks. Ask: are there critical buffers that are too small or too large?

**Level 10 - Stock-and-Flow Structures**
Physical infrastructure, organizational structures. Hard to change but powerful. Ask: does the physical or organizational structure constrain desired behavior?

**Level 9 - Delays**
Time lags in feedback loops. Long delays cause oscillation and instability. Ask: are there delays that prevent the system from responding to feedback?

**Level 8 - Balancing Feedback Loops**
Mechanisms that correct deviations. Ask: are there balancing loops that should exist but do not? Are existing ones too weak?

**Level 7 - Reinforcing Feedback Loops**
Mechanisms that amplify change. Ask: are there reinforcing loops driving undesired growth or decline? Can new ones be created for desired outcomes?

**Level 6 - Information Flows**
Who has access to what information. Ask: is critical information missing from key decision-makers? Would transparency change behavior?

**Level 5 - Rules**
Incentives, punishments, constraints. Ask: do the rules reward desired behavior or undesired behavior? What rule changes would realign incentives?

**Level 4 - Self-Organization**
The ability of the system to create new structures and behaviors. Ask: can the system adapt and evolve, or is it rigid? How can adaptive capacity be increased?

**Level 3 - Goals**
The purpose of the system. Ask: is the system optimizing for the right goal? Would changing the stated goal change behavior?

**Level 2 - Paradigms**
The shared mental models and assumptions that underlie the system. Ask: what beliefs and assumptions are taken for granted? What would change if those assumptions were challenged?

**Level 1 - Transcending Paradigms** (strongest leverage)
The ability to operate from multiple paradigms and choose among them. This is the deepest leverage but the hardest to implement.

### Step 3: Candidate Leverage Point Evaluation
For each identified candidate, assess:
- **Impact**: How much system behavior change would this produce?
- **Feasibility**: How difficult is this intervention to implement?
- **Resistance**: How much opposition will this intervention face?
- **Speed**: How quickly would effects be seen?
- **Reversibility**: Can this be undone if it does not work?

### Step 4: Prioritization
Rank leverage points by impact-to-effort ratio. Note that higher-level leverage points (rules, goals, paradigms) are more powerful but harder to implement. Lower-level points (parameters, buffers) are easier but less transformative.

### Step 5: Intervention Design
For the top-priority leverage points, design specific interventions. Include unintended consequence analysis using the causal map.

## Outputs

- A list of identified leverage points organized by Meadows' hierarchy level.
- Evaluation of each leverage point on impact, feasibility, resistance, speed, and reversibility.
- A prioritized shortlist of recommended intervention points.
- Specific intervention designs for top-priority leverage points.
- Risk assessment for each proposed intervention.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Parameter fixation**: Spending all effort on Level 12 (tweaking numbers) when deeper structural changes are needed.
- **Paradigm fantasy**: Proposing Level 1-2 changes without a realistic path to implementation.
- **Ignoring resistance**: Identifying a powerful leverage point but underestimating the forces that will resist change.
- **Single-point thinking**: Focusing on one leverage point when the system requires coordinated intervention at multiple levels.
- **Mechanical application**: Applying Meadows' hierarchy as a rigid checklist rather than a thinking tool.
- **Overlooking unintended consequences**: Intervening at a leverage point without tracing the effects through the full system.
