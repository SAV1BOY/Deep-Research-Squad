# Semantic Pivoting Framework

## Purpose
Provide structured techniques for redirecting search strategy when initial queries fail to return useful results. Instead of repeating failed searches with minor variations, semantic pivoting moves to fundamentally different conceptual territories that may contain the needed evidence.

## When to Use
- When initial queries and their expansions return poor or no results.
- When the topic may be discussed under entirely different conceptual framing in other fields.
- When direct evidence is scarce but analogous evidence from related domains may exist.
- When the terminology used by the researcher does not match how practitioners or academics discuss the topic.

## Inputs
- Failed or low-yield queries and their results.
- The underlying research question (not just the query terms).
- Domain context and any partial findings.
- The type of evidence sought (data, opinion, analysis, primary source).

## Process

### Step 1: Diagnose the Failure
- Classify the failure type:
  - **No results**: The topic may not be discussed in these terms.
  - **Irrelevant results**: The terms are ambiguous or used differently in another context.
  - **Too broad results**: The query lacks specificity.
  - **Outdated results**: The terminology has evolved.
- The diagnosis determines which pivot technique to apply.

### Step 2: Pivot to Broader Terms
- Move up one level of abstraction.
- Replace specific terms with their parent categories.
- Example: "transformer architecture attention scaling" becomes "neural network efficiency."
- Use this when the specific topic is too niche for available sources.

### Step 3: Pivot to Narrower Terms
- Move down one level of specificity.
- Replace general terms with concrete instances or examples.
- Example: "supply chain disruption" becomes "semiconductor chip shortage Taiwan 2021."
- Use this when broad queries return too much noise.

### Step 4: Pivot to Related Fields
- Identify which other disciplines study the same phenomenon under different names.
- Example: "customer churn prediction" in business maps to "survival analysis" in statistics.
- Consult interdisciplinary dictionaries or ask: "What would a [physicist/economist/sociologist] call this?"

### Step 5: Pivot to Analogous Domains
- Find domains where a structurally similar problem has been studied more extensively.
- Example: Studying organizational resilience? Look at ecological resilience research.
- Map the analogy explicitly: what corresponds to what between domains.

### Step 6: Pivot by Perspective
- Search for the same topic from a different stakeholder's viewpoint.
- Example: Instead of "impact of regulation on fintech," try "fintech compliance challenges."
- Different stakeholders use different vocabulary for the same phenomenon.

### Step 7: Pivot by Time or Geography
- Search for the same phenomenon in a different era or location where it may be better documented.
- Example: Current AI regulation may be poorly documented, but historical internet regulation may offer precedents.

### Step 8: Evaluate and Iterate
- After each pivot, assess whether results have improved.
- If one pivot direction shows promise, explore it further before trying others.
- Document which pivots worked and which did not for future reference.

## Outputs
- A pivot log documenting: original query, failure diagnosis, pivot technique applied, new query, result quality.
- Revised query set for continued investigation.
- Cross-domain mappings discovered during pivoting.

## Common Pitfalls
- Pivoting too quickly without fully exploring the original semantic space.
- Pivoting without diagnosing the failure, leading to random rather than strategic redirects.
- Losing track of the original research question during aggressive pivoting.
- Failing to map analogies rigorously, leading to false parallels.
- Not documenting pivots, causing repeated dead ends.

## Related Frameworks
- **Query Expansion**: Try expansion before pivoting; pivoting is for when expansion fails.
- **Keyword Grid**: Pivoting may reveal new dimensions for the grid.
- **Source Class Routing**: A pivot may require switching source classes entirely.
- **Operator Matrix**: New platforms may require different search operators.
- **Issue Tree**: Pivoting may reveal that the issue tree needs restructuring.
