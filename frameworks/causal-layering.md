# Causal Layering Framework

## Purpose
Analyze research topics at multiple depths rather than settling for surface-level
explanations. Based on Sohail Inayatullah's Causal Layered Analysis (CLA),
adapted for research contexts. This framework moves from observable symptoms
through mechanisms and causes to the deep structural and contextual conditions
that shape outcomes.

## When to Use
- Surface-level answers feel incomplete or unsatisfying.
- The research question asks "why" something happens.
- Stakeholders need to understand root causes, not just descriptions.
- The topic involves systemic or recurring patterns.
- Solutions to a problem keep failing because they address symptoms only.
- Complex phenomena require multi-level explanation.

## Inputs
- The phenomenon, trend, or problem to analyze.
- Available evidence and data at all levels.
- Domain knowledge about the system in question.
- Historical context and structural information.

## Process

### Layer 1: Symptom (What We See)
**The observable, measurable surface.**
- What are the visible events, data points, or outcomes?
- What does the phenomenon look like from the outside?
- What would a news headline say about it?
- Capture quantitative data: numbers, trends, measurements.
- Document qualitative observations: what people report seeing.

Questions to ask:
- What is actually happening, stripped of interpretation?
- What data confirms these observations?
- When did this first become visible?
- Who is affected and how?

### Layer 2: Mechanism (How It Works)
**The processes and systems that produce the symptoms.**
- What causal chains connect inputs to the observed symptoms?
- What feedback loops are operating?
- What are the proximate causes (the immediate "how")?
- Map the sequence of events from trigger to symptom.
- Identify the actors, processes, and interactions involved.

Questions to ask:
- What process produces this outcome step by step?
- What are the inputs and outputs at each stage?
- Where are the bottlenecks, amplifiers, or dampeners?
- What happens if one part of the mechanism is removed?

### Layer 3: Cause (Why It Happens)
**The driving forces behind the mechanisms.**
- What motivations, incentives, or pressures drive the mechanisms?
- What decisions or policies created the current system?
- What historical events set this in motion?
- Distinguish between necessary causes and sufficient causes.
- Identify whether causes are proximate or distal.

Questions to ask:
- Why does this mechanism exist in the first place?
- Whose interests does it serve?
- What would need to change for the mechanism to stop operating?
- Is this cause necessary (without it, no symptom) or contributing
  (makes symptom more likely)?

### Layer 4: Structure (Systemic Conditions)
**The deeper structural patterns that enable the causes.**
- What institutional, economic, or social structures make these causes possible?
- What power dynamics, resource distributions, or organizational designs
  are in play?
- What rules, norms, or regulations shape behavior?
- What structural constraints limit the range of possible outcomes?
- Are there paradigms or mental models that perpetuate the structure?

Questions to ask:
- What system is this phenomenon embedded in?
- What are the rules (formal and informal) that govern this system?
- Who designed these structures, and for what purpose?
- What alternative structures exist elsewhere?

### Layer 5: Context (Environment)
**The broadest environmental and cultural conditions.**
- What cultural, technological, economic, or environmental forces shape
  the entire landscape?
- What era-specific conditions make this situation possible?
- What global or civilizational trends are relevant?
- What worldviews, values, or ideologies underpin the structures?
- What would be different if this existed in another time or culture?

Questions to ask:
- What larger trends make this possible or inevitable?
- What cultural narratives support or challenge this phenomenon?
- How would this look in a fundamentally different context?
- What long-term forces are shaping this trajectory?

### Step 6: Cross-Layer Synthesis
- Trace causal chains from Context down through Structure, Cause,
  Mechanism, to Symptom.
- Identify which layer is most amenable to intervention.
- Note where deeper layers explain why surface interventions fail.
- Assess which layers have the strongest evidence and which are more
  speculative.
- Determine the appropriate depth for the research output.

## Outputs
- Five-layer analysis document with findings at each level.
- Causal chain diagram linking layers.
- Identification of the deepest well-evidenced cause.
- Assessment of which layer is most relevant for the research question.
- Recommendations for which layer to target for intervention (if applicable).
- Confidence assessment per layer (deeper layers are typically more speculative).

## Common Pitfalls
- **Surface fixation**: Stopping at Layer 1 or 2 and declaring the analysis
  complete. Always push to at least Layer 3.
- **Depth fetishism**: Assuming deeper layers are always more important or
  more "true." Sometimes the mechanism layer is what matters most.
- **Speculation creep**: Deeper layers often have weaker evidence. Clearly
  mark where evidence ends and interpretation begins.
- **Monocausal thinking**: Assuming each layer has a single element. Real
  systems have multiple symptoms, mechanisms, causes, and structures.
- **Layer confusion**: Misclassifying elements. A common error is listing
  a mechanism as a cause. Mechanisms describe how; causes explain why.
- **Missing feedback loops**: Symptoms can reinforce causes, and structures
  can be changed by outcomes. The layers interact bidirectionally.

## Related Frameworks
- **First-Principles Research**: Complements Layer 3-5 analysis by stripping
  away assumptions to find fundamental truths.
- **Question Decomposition**: Use to break each layer into researchable sub-questions.
- **Evidence Ladder**: Apply to evidence at each layer; deeper layers often
  have weaker evidence types.
- **Unknowns and Assumptions**: Deeper layers contain more assumptions; use
  this framework to surface and classify them.

## Templates

### Causal Layer Analysis Template
```
PHENOMENON: [What is being analyzed]

LAYER 1 - SYMPTOM (What We See):
- Observable: [Data, events, trends]
- Evidence: [Sources and strength]

LAYER 2 - MECHANISM (How It Works):
- Process: [Step-by-step causal chain]
- Feedback loops: [Any self-reinforcing dynamics]
- Evidence: [Sources and strength]

LAYER 3 - CAUSE (Why It Happens):
- Driving forces: [Motivations, incentives, decisions]
- Necessary vs contributing: [Classification]
- Evidence: [Sources and strength]

LAYER 4 - STRUCTURE (Systemic Conditions):
- Systems: [Institutional, economic, social structures]
- Rules and norms: [Formal and informal constraints]
- Evidence: [Sources and strength]

LAYER 5 - CONTEXT (Environment):
- Trends: [Cultural, technological, global forces]
- Worldviews: [Underlying values and paradigms]
- Evidence: [Sources and strength]

CROSS-LAYER CHAIN:
[Context] -> [Structure] -> [Cause] -> [Mechanism] -> [Symptom]

DEEPEST WELL-EVIDENCED LAYER: [Layer N]
MOST ACTIONABLE LAYER: [Layer N]
KEY INSIGHT: [What the layered analysis reveals that surface analysis misses]
```
