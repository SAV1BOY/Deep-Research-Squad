# Implications Mapping Framework

## Purpose

Map the implications of research findings across stakeholders, time horizons, and orders of effect. Answer the questions: Who is affected? How? When? What should they do? Track first-order effects (direct and immediate), second-order effects (indirect consequences), and third-order effects (systemic ripples).

## When to Use

- When research findings have consequences for multiple groups or domains.
- When decision-makers need to understand the full ripple effects of a finding or decision.
- When assessing whether an action will have unintended consequences.
- When communicating complex, interconnected impacts to diverse stakeholders.

## Inputs

- Research findings or conclusions to be mapped.
- A list of stakeholders or affected parties.
- Understanding of the system or environment in which effects will propagate.
- Time horizon for the analysis (short-term, medium-term, long-term).

## Process

### Step 1: Finding Identification
State each finding or conclusion that will be mapped. Be precise and specific. Vague findings produce vague implications.

### Step 2: Stakeholder Inventory
List all parties who could be affected. Categories to consider:
- Direct participants (users, customers, employees).
- Adjacent parties (competitors, suppliers, partners).
- Oversight bodies (regulators, auditors, governance).
- Broader society (communities, public, environment).

### Step 3: First-Order Effects
For each finding and each stakeholder, identify the direct, immediate impact:
- What changes for this stakeholder as a direct result?
- How soon does the effect manifest?
- How significant is the effect (minor inconvenience vs. fundamental change)?
- Is the effect positive, negative, or neutral for this stakeholder?

### Step 4: Second-Order Effects
For each first-order effect, ask: what happens next?
- How will the stakeholder respond to the first-order effect?
- What does their response cause for other stakeholders?
- What feedback loops are created?
- What resources are redirected?

### Step 5: Third-Order Effects
For each significant second-order effect, extend the chain one more step:
- What systemic changes emerge from accumulated second-order effects?
- What norms, markets, or structures shift?
- What new equilibrium might form?
- Flag: third-order effects carry high uncertainty. Label them explicitly as speculative.

### Step 6: Temporal Mapping
Organize all effects along a timeline:
- **Immediate** (days to weeks): First-order effects that manifest quickly.
- **Near-term** (weeks to months): First-order effects with lag, early second-order effects.
- **Medium-term** (months to a year): Second-order effects, emerging third-order effects.
- **Long-term** (year-plus): Third-order effects, systemic shifts.

### Step 7: Response Recommendations
For each significantly affected stakeholder, suggest appropriate responses:
- What should they do to capitalize on positive effects?
- What should they do to mitigate negative effects?
- What should they monitor as leading indicators?
- When should they act versus wait for more information?

### Step 8: Interaction Effects
Examine how effects on different stakeholders interact:
- Do positive effects for one group create negative effects for another?
- Are there win-win opportunities where aligned action benefits multiple parties?
- Where do conflicts of interest emerge?

## Outputs

- An implications map organized by stakeholder and order of effect.
- A temporal timeline showing when effects are expected to manifest.
- Response recommendations for each significantly affected stakeholder.
- Identification of interaction effects, conflicts, and win-win opportunities.
- Uncertainty flags on all second-order and third-order effects.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Stopping at first order**: Most important consequences are second-order or third-order. Push past the obvious.
- **Stakeholder blindness**: Forgetting a key affected party because they are not in the room or not the primary audience.
- **Optimism bias**: Mapping positive implications thoroughly while underexploring negative ones, or vice versa.
- **False precision on timing**: Stating "this will happen in Q3" when the honest answer is "sometime in the next year."
- **Ignoring feedback loops**: Treating effects as one-directional when stakeholder responses create circular dynamics.
- **Effect inflation**: Claiming massive third-order effects from minor findings. Maintain proportionality.
