# Deep Dive Narrative Framework

## Purpose

Structure a comprehensive research narrative that takes the reader from initial context through investigation to findings and actionable recommendations. This format provides rich detail, evidence links, and thorough analysis for audiences who need the full picture, not just the summary.

## When to Use

- When the audience wants to understand not just the answer but the reasoning and evidence behind it.
- When the topic is complex enough that a summary would lose critical nuance.
- When the research will be referenced repeatedly as a knowledge resource.
- When accountability requires showing the full analytical process.

## Inputs

- Complete research findings with source citations.
- The original research question and its context.
- All evidence gathered, including contradictory or inconclusive material.
- Analysis notes, working hypotheses, and how they evolved during research.

## Process

### Step 1: Context Section
Set the stage for the reader. Answer these questions:
- Why does this research matter? What is at stake?
- What is the background the reader needs to understand the problem?
- What has been tried or said about this topic before?
- Keep this section concise. It is setup, not the main event.

### Step 2: Problem Definition Section
State the specific question or problem being investigated. Make it precise:
- What exactly are we trying to answer or solve?
- What are the boundaries of the investigation (scope)?
- What is excluded and why?
- What would a successful answer look like?

### Step 3: Investigation Section
Describe how the research was conducted. This builds credibility:
- What sources were consulted and why those sources?
- What methods were used (data analysis, expert interviews, literature review)?
- What challenges or obstacles were encountered?
- How were conflicting sources or data resolved?

### Step 4: Findings Section
Present the core discoveries. Structure findings by theme, not by source:
- Group related findings together under descriptive subheadings.
- Lead each finding with the conclusion, then provide supporting evidence.
- Link every claim to its source.
- Present contradictory evidence honestly rather than hiding it.
- Use data visualizations, tables, or structured comparisons where they add clarity.

### Step 5: Implications Section
Explain what the findings mean. Move from description to interpretation:
- What do the findings suggest about the broader question?
- Who is affected and how?
- What are the short-term and long-term implications?
- What new questions do the findings raise?

### Step 6: Recommendations Section
Based on the implications, state what should be done:
- Prioritize recommendations by impact and feasibility.
- For each recommendation: what, who, when, and what resources are needed.
- Distinguish between "must do" and "should consider" recommendations.
- Identify risks associated with each recommendation.

### Step 7: Evidence Appendix
Compile detailed evidence that supports the narrative but would disrupt its flow:
- Raw data tables, full quotations, methodology details.
- This section is for reference, not for sequential reading.

### Step 8: Narrative Review
Read the entire document as a continuous narrative. It should flow logically from section to section without gaps or redundancies.

## Outputs

- A structured document with six main sections: Context, Problem, Investigation, Findings, Implications, Recommendations.
- An optional Evidence Appendix for detailed supporting material.
- In-text source citations throughout.
- A length appropriate to the complexity of the topic, typically 2,000-10,000 words.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Methodology overemphasis**: The Investigation section should build trust, not dominate the document. Keep it proportional.
- **Findings without interpretation**: Listing facts without explaining what they mean leaves the reader to guess.
- **Implications without evidence**: Making claims in the Implications section that are not grounded in the Findings section.
- **Burying contradictions**: Hiding conflicting evidence undermines credibility when discovered. Present it openly.
- **Recommendation timidity**: Ending with "more research is needed" when the evidence supports actionable recommendations.
- **Narrative drift**: Losing the thread of the central question. Every section should connect back to the problem statement.
