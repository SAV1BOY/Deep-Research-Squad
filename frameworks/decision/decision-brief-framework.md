# Decision Brief Framework

## Purpose
A structured template for presenting research findings as a decision-ready brief. Transforms analysis into a concise document that gives decision-makers the context, options, analysis, recommendation, and risk assessment they need to act with confidence.

## When to Use
- When research has reached the point where a decision is needed
- When presenting findings to executives, boards, or senior leadership
- When multiple options exist and a recommended path must be articulated
- When the decision has significant consequences and needs a formal record

## Inputs
- Synthesized research findings from the Layered Synthesis framework
- Implications mapped across stakeholders and time horizons
- Options identified during analysis
- Confidence levels and evidence ratings for key findings

## Process

### 1. Context
Set the stage in one page or less:
- What prompted this research and this decision?
- What is the current situation and why does it require action?
- What has changed or what new information has emerged?
- What is the decision window and what happens if no decision is made?
- Keep this factual and free of advocacy

### 2. Options
Present the viable options clearly:
- List three to five options including "do nothing" or "status quo" as a baseline
- Describe each option in two to four sentences: what it involves, what it requires, and what it assumes
- Ensure options are genuinely distinct, not variations of the same approach
- Exclude options already ruled out and explain briefly why they were eliminated

### 3. Analysis
Evaluate each option against consistent criteria:
- Define three to five evaluation criteria relevant to the decision (cost, speed, risk, impact, feasibility)
- Assess each option against each criterion with evidence citations
- Use the Confidence Map ratings to indicate certainty of each assessment
- Highlight trade-offs: where does each option excel and where does it fall short?
- Present the analysis in a comparable format (table or structured list)

### 4. Recommendation
State the recommended option and the reasoning:
- Lead with the recommendation, then explain why
- Trace the logic from evidence through analysis to the recommendation
- Acknowledge what would need to be true for this recommendation to be wrong
- State the confidence level of the recommendation
- If no single option is clearly superior, say so and explain the deciding factors

### 5. Risks
Identify risks associated with the recommended option:
- List the top three to five risks with likelihood and impact assessments
- For each risk, propose a mitigation strategy
- Identify risks common to all options versus risks specific to the recommendation
- Note any irreversible consequences
- State what monitoring or checkpoints should be established post-decision

### 6. Next Steps
If the recommendation is accepted:
- What are the immediate actions required?
- Who is responsible for each action?
- What is the timeline?
- When should the decision be revisited?

## Outputs
- A concise decision brief (typically three to five pages)
- A clear recommendation with supporting reasoning
- Risk assessment with mitigation strategies
- An actionable next-steps plan

## Common Pitfalls
- **Burying the recommendation**: Decision-makers want the answer first, then the reasoning. Lead with the recommendation.
- **Too many options**: More than five options causes decision paralysis. Consolidate similar options and cut clearly inferior ones.
- **Advocacy disguised as analysis**: The analysis section must be even-handed. Save advocacy for the recommendation section.
- **Ignoring "do nothing"**: Always include the status quo option. Sometimes it is the right choice, and it provides a baseline for comparison.
- **Vague risks**: "Things could go wrong" is not a risk assessment. Be specific about what could go wrong, how likely it is, and what to do about it.
- **Missing the decision window**: A brilliant brief delivered after the decision window closes is worthless. Prioritize timeliness.
