# Recommendation Stack

## Purpose
A prioritization framework for organizing recommendations into clear action tiers. Prevents the common problem of presenting a flat list of recommendations with no guidance on sequencing or importance. Gives decision-makers a structured view of what to do first, what to do next, and what to watch.

## When to Use
- When research produces multiple recommendations that compete for attention and resources
- When presenting to decision-makers who need clear prioritization guidance
- When resources are limited and not all recommendations can be implemented simultaneously
- When recommendations span different time horizons and commitment levels

## Inputs
- Recommendations from the Layered Synthesis or Decision Brief
- Confidence levels for each recommendation
- Resource constraints (budget, time, personnel, political capital)
- Stakeholder priorities and urgency factors
- Dependencies between recommendations

## Process

### 1. Must-Do (Critical Priority)
Recommendations that require immediate action:
- Failure to act creates significant risk or missed irreversible opportunity
- Supported by high-confidence evidence
- Clear causal link between action and outcome
- Consequences of inaction are well understood and unacceptable
- Typically one to three items; more than three dilutes urgency
- For each: state the action, the deadline, the owner, and the consequence of inaction

### 2. Should-Do (High Priority)
Recommendations that are strongly supported but less time-critical:
- Important for achieving strategic objectives
- Supported by medium to high confidence evidence
- Benefits are significant but the window for action is wider
- May require more planning or resources than must-do items
- Typically three to five items
- For each: state the action, the recommended timeline, and the expected benefit

### 3. Could-Do (Moderate Priority)
Recommendations that add value but are discretionary:
- Would improve outcomes but are not essential
- May be supported by lower-confidence evidence or have uncertain ROI
- Can be deferred without significant negative consequences
- Often contingent on must-do and should-do items being completed first
- Typically three to seven items
- For each: state the action, the conditions under which it becomes higher priority, and the estimated value

### 4. Monitor (Watch List)
Items that do not require action now but should be tracked:
- Emerging trends or signals that could trigger future action
- Areas where evidence is currently insufficient for a recommendation
- Risks that are low probability but high impact
- Competitor or market movements that could change the calculus
- For each: state what to watch for, how to monitor it, and what trigger would move it to a higher tier

### 5. Dependencies and Sequencing
Map the relationships between recommendations:
- Which must-do items enable should-do items?
- Which could-do items become should-do items if certain conditions are met?
- What is the optimal implementation sequence considering dependencies?
- Where can recommendations be pursued in parallel versus sequentially?

### 6. Resource Mapping
For each tier, estimate the resource requirements:
- What investment of time, money, and attention does each tier require?
- Does the organization have capacity for the must-do and should-do tiers?
- If resources are insufficient, what trade-offs are recommended?
- Where can quick wins be captured with minimal resource expenditure?

## Outputs
- A four-tier recommendation stack with clear prioritization
- Specific actions, owners, and timelines for must-do and should-do tiers
- Dependency map showing implementation sequencing
- Resource estimates per tier
- A monitoring plan for the watch list

## Common Pitfalls
- **Everything is must-do**: If everything is critical, nothing is. Limit must-do to genuine emergencies. Force prioritization.
- **No owner assigned**: Recommendations without clear ownership do not get implemented. Name the responsible party.
- **Ignoring dependencies**: Recommending an action that depends on a prior action without noting the dependency causes implementation failure.
- **Empty monitor tier**: If nothing is on the watch list, you are not thinking about emerging risks and opportunities.
- **Static stack**: The stack should be revisited as conditions change. A should-do item may become must-do when new information arrives.
- **Ignoring resource reality**: A recommendation stack that exceeds available resources is a wish list, not a plan. Be realistic about capacity.
