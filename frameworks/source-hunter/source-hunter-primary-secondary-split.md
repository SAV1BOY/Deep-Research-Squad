# Primary-Secondary Source Split Framework

## Purpose
Classify every source encountered during research into primary, secondary, or tertiary categories. This classification determines how the source should be weighted, how it should be cited, and what verification it requires before being used as evidence.

## When to Use
- When evaluating any source before incorporating it into research findings.
- When building evidence for a claim and needing to assess evidence strength.
- When the research requires tracing claims back to their origin.
- When advising other agents on which sources to prioritize.

## Inputs
- A source to be classified (document, dataset, article, report, testimony).
- Context about the research question and what type of evidence is needed.
- Information about the source's provenance (who created it, when, why).

## Process

### Step 1: Identify Primary Sources
Primary sources provide direct, first-hand evidence. They are:
- Original datasets, raw data, and statistical tables.
- Direct observations, fieldwork notes, and lab results.
- Official records: court filings, legislation, patents, regulatory filings.
- First-person accounts: interviews, testimonies, diaries, correspondence.
- Original creative or technical works: source code, architectural plans, original research papers presenting new findings.
- Company filings: 10-K, annual reports, earnings transcripts.
- Surveys and census data in their original form.

### Step 2: Identify Secondary Sources
Secondary sources analyze, interpret, or discuss primary sources. They are:
- Research papers that analyze or synthesize others' data.
- News articles reporting on events or studies.
- Textbooks and educational materials.
- Review articles and meta-analyses.
- Analyst reports and market research.
- Biographies and historical analyses.
- Commentary, editorials, and opinion pieces backed by evidence.

### Step 3: Identify Tertiary Sources
Tertiary sources compile and summarize secondary sources. They are:
- Encyclopedias (including Wikipedia).
- Directories and databases.
- Fact sheets and summaries.
- Bibliographies and indexes.
- Guidebooks and handbooks.
- Almanacs and chronologies.

### Step 4: Determine Appropriate Use by Category

| Category | Use For | Weight in Evidence | Verification Need |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | Direct evidence, ground truth, original data | Highest | Verify authenticity and provenance |
| Secondary | Context, interpretation, expert analysis | Medium | Verify it accurately represents primary sources |
| Tertiary | Orientation, finding primary/secondary sources | Lowest | Do not use as final evidence; use to discover better sources |

### Step 5: Handle Ambiguous Cases
- A source can be primary in one context and secondary in another. A newspaper article is primary evidence of public discourse but secondary evidence of the event it reports.
- When ambiguous, classify based on how the source is being used in this specific research.
- Document the classification rationale for ambiguous cases.

### Step 6: Set Collection Priorities
- For claims that are central to the research, require at least one primary source.
- For contextual information, secondary sources are acceptable.
- Never let tertiary sources be the sole evidence for any claim in the final output.

## Outputs
- A classification label (primary, secondary, tertiary) for each source.
- A rationale for the classification, especially for ambiguous cases.
- Guidance on how each source should be weighted in the evidence assessment.
- Flags for claims that lack primary source support.

## Common Pitfalls
- Treating all published sources as equally authoritative regardless of category.
- Using tertiary sources as evidence instead of as discovery tools.
- Failing to recognize that a source's classification depends on its use context.
- Assuming secondary sources accurately represent primary sources without checking.
- Over-relying on secondary sources because they are more accessible than primary ones.
- Classifying opinion pieces or commentary as primary sources.

## Related Frameworks
- **Authority Proximity**: Refines the evaluation within each source category.
- **Publication Chain**: Traces the chain from primary through secondary to tertiary.
- **Signal-Noise Scoring**: Source category feeds into the overall quality score.
- **Citation Integrity**: Verifies that secondary sources faithfully represent their primary sources.
- **Claim Grid**: Source category determines the evidence strength column.
