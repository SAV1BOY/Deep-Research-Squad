# Data Quality Assessment Framework

## Purpose
A structured methodology for evaluating the quality of data sources across multiple dimensions before incorporating them into research findings. Ensures that conclusions are built on data whose strengths and limitations are explicitly understood, documented, and communicated.

## When to Apply
- When incorporating quantitative data from unfamiliar or unvetted sources
- When multiple data sources report conflicting figures for the same phenomenon
- When research conclusions will drive high-stakes decisions
- When aggregating data across different collection methodologies or jurisdictions
- Before citing any statistic as a key finding
- When a data source has changed ownership, methodology, or scope since it was last used

## Core Principles
1. **Quality is multidimensional**: No single metric captures data quality. Accuracy, completeness, timeliness, consistency, and provenance each matter independently.
2. **Fitness for purpose over abstract perfection**: Data that is imperfect in general may be perfectly adequate for the specific research question at hand.
3. **Transparency over gatekeeping**: The goal is not to reject imperfect data but to document its limitations so consumers can calibrate their confidence appropriately.
4. **Upstream matters**: Data quality problems usually originate in collection and processing, not in the dataset itself. Trace the pipeline.
5. **Degradation is normal**: Data quality changes over time. A source that was reliable last year may have changed methodology, coverage, or ownership.

## Step-by-Step Methodology

### Step 1: Identify the Data Source and Its Provenance
Document who collected the data, when, how, and for what original purpose. Key question: Was this data collected to answer a question similar to ours, or are we repurposing it?

### Step 2: Assess Accuracy
Compare values against known benchmarks, cross-reference sources, or check internal consistency. Key question: Where we can verify, do the values match reality?

### Step 3: Evaluate Completeness
Determine what coverage the data provides and what is missing. Key question: What populations, time periods, or categories are absent, and could their absence bias findings?

### Step 4: Check Timeliness
Assess whether the data reflects current conditions or a historical snapshot. Key question: Has the underlying phenomenon changed materially since this data was collected?

### Step 5: Examine Consistency
Look for internal contradictions, unit mismatches, or definitional shifts across the dataset. Key question: Are the same terms measured the same way throughout?

### Step 6: Evaluate Methodology Transparency
Determine whether the collection methodology is documented, reproducible, and appropriate. Key question: Could an independent researcher replicate this data collection?

### Step 7: Assess Bias and Collection Context
Evaluate whether the data collection process introduces systematic bias. Key question: Who collected this data, what incentives did they have, and could those incentives have influenced what was measured or how?

### Step 8: Assign a Quality Rating
Score each dimension (accuracy, completeness, timeliness, consistency, provenance, bias) and produce an overall fitness-for-purpose rating. Key question: Given these scores, what caveats must accompany any findings based on this data?

## Output Format
- Data source identification card (name, publisher, date, methodology summary)
- Dimension-by-dimension quality scores (high, adequate, low, unknown) with justifications
- Overall fitness-for-purpose rating for the specific research question
- Required caveats and limitations to include in any reporting
- Recommendations for supplementary data sources to fill identified gaps

## Integration with Other Frameworks
- **Benchmarking Methodology Framework**: Quality assessment must precede any benchmarking exercise.
- **Evidence Rating**: Data quality scores feed into broader evidence quality ratings.
- **Source Reliability**: Data quality is one component of overall source reliability.
- **Confidence Weighting**: Low data quality should directly reduce confidence in dependent findings.
- **Statistical Literacy**: Understanding methodology issues requires statistical reasoning.

## Anti-Patterns
- **Authority substitution**: Assuming data is high quality because it comes from a prestigious institution without actually checking.
- **Completeness blindness**: Using a dataset without asking what it does not cover, then generalizing findings as if coverage were universal.
- **Vintage ignorance**: Citing outdated data without noting its age or assessing whether conditions have changed.
- **Methodology hand-waving**: Accepting "survey data" or "proprietary model" as sufficient methodology description.
- **Binary quality judgment**: Treating data as either "good enough to use" or "too flawed to use" when the correct response is usually "usable with stated caveats."
- **Ignoring collection incentives**: Failing to consider whether the data collector had incentives that could introduce systematic bias into the dataset.
