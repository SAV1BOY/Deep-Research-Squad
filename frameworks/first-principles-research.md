# First-Principles Research Framework

## Purpose
Reduce any research topic to its fundamental, self-evident truths, then rebuild
understanding from those foundations. This prevents accepting inherited assumptions,
conventional wisdom, or surface-level explanations as fact.

## When to Use
- Conflicting sources make it unclear what is actually true.
- The topic is surrounded by hype, ideology, or marketing.
- Conventional explanations feel incomplete or circular.
- You need to evaluate a novel claim with no established consensus.
- Research keeps returning opinions rather than evidence.

## Inputs
- The claim, topic, or question to analyze.
- Any existing explanations or conventional narratives.
- Available evidence and source material.
- Domain constraints and known physical/logical laws.

## Process

### Step 1: Identify the Claim
- State the claim or topic precisely and unambiguously.
- Separate the core assertion from its framing and rhetoric.
- Note who is making the claim and in what context.
- Distinguish between descriptive claims (what is) and normative claims (what should be).

### Step 2: Strip Away Assumptions
- List every assumption the claim relies on, both explicit and implicit.
- For each assumption, ask: "Is this necessarily true, or just commonly accepted?"
- Categorize assumptions as: axiomatic (self-evident), empirical (testable),
  conventional (accepted by tradition), or speculative (unverified).
- Discard conventional and speculative assumptions temporarily.

### Step 3: Find the Bedrock Facts
- Identify what remains after assumption removal: these are candidate fundamentals.
- For each candidate, verify it through independent evidence.
- Apply the "five whys" technique: keep asking why until you reach a foundational truth.
- Bedrock facts must be: independently verifiable, not dependent on authority,
  consistent with established physical/logical laws.
- Document each bedrock fact with its supporting evidence.

### Step 4: Rebuild Understanding
- Starting only from verified bedrock facts, reconstruct the explanation.
- Add complexity one layer at a time, justifying each addition.
- At each layer, ask: "Does this necessarily follow from what we know?"
- Note where the reconstruction diverges from the conventional narrative.
- Mark any point where you must add an assumption to proceed.

### Step 5: Test Against Evidence
- Compare the rebuilt understanding against available empirical evidence.
- Identify predictions the rebuilt model makes that differ from conventional views.
- Check if evidence supports the rebuilt understanding or the conventional one.
- Look for edge cases and counterexamples that stress-test the model.
- Assign confidence based on how well the rebuilt model survives testing.

## Outputs
- List of bedrock facts with evidence sources.
- Rebuilt explanation constructed from fundamentals.
- Comparison between first-principles understanding and conventional narrative.
- Identified assumptions that were discarded and why.
- Confidence assessment of the rebuilt understanding.
- List of remaining uncertainties and untestable assumptions.

## Common Pitfalls
- **False fundamentals**: Treating deeply embedded assumptions as bedrock truths.
  Always verify with independent evidence.
- **Infinite regress**: Going too deep and questioning things that are genuinely
  axiomatic (e.g., basic logic, mathematics). Know when to stop.
- **Reconstruction bias**: Unconsciously rebuilding toward the conclusion you
  expected. Let the fundamentals lead wherever they go.
- **Ignoring complexity**: Real systems have emergent properties that cannot be
  predicted from fundamentals alone. First principles are a starting point,
  not the complete picture.
- **Authority rejection fallacy**: Dismissing expert consensus just because it
  is conventional. Conventional can also be correct.
- **Oversimplification**: Stripping away so many assumptions that the remaining
  model is too simple to be useful.

## Related Frameworks
- **Question Decomposition**: Breaks the topic into manageable pieces before
  applying first-principles analysis.
- **Evidence Ladder**: Helps evaluate the strength of evidence found at Step 3.
- **Claim-to-Evidence Chain**: Ensures each rebuilt layer has proper evidence support.
- **Unknowns and Assumptions**: Provides structured categorization for Step 2.

### First-Principles Analysis Template
```
CLAIM: [Precise statement of the claim]
SOURCE: [Who makes this claim and in what context]

ASSUMPTIONS IDENTIFIED:
- [Assumption 1]: [axiomatic | empirical | conventional | speculative]
- [Assumption 2]: [axiomatic | empirical | conventional | speculative]

BEDROCK FACTS:
1. [Fact]: [Evidence source] - [Verification method]
2. [Fact]: [Evidence source] - [Verification method]

REBUILT UNDERSTANDING:
Layer 1: [Direct conclusion from bedrock facts]
Layer 2: [Built on Layer 1 + additional justified reasoning]
Layer 3: [Built on Layer 2 + any necessary assumptions noted]

DIVERGENCE FROM CONVENTIONAL VIEW:
- [Where and why the rebuilt understanding differs]

CONFIDENCE: [Low | Medium | High | Very High]
REASONING: [Why this confidence level]
```
