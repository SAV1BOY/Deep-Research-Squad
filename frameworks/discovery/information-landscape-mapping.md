# Information Landscape Mapping

## Purpose
A method for surveying and documenting the full terrain of available information on a research topic before deep investigation begins. Produces a map showing where information is abundant, where it is sparse, where it is contested, and where it does not exist, enabling strategic allocation of research effort and honest scoping of what the research can deliver.

## When to Apply
- At the outset of a new research topic where the information ecosystem is unfamiliar
- When stakeholders assume information is readily available but it may not be
- When research spans multiple domains with different information cultures and norms
- When planning resource allocation across research sub-questions
- When prior research efforts failed because they searched in the wrong places
- When the research question is interdisciplinary and information may live in unexpected source ecosystems

## Core Principles
1. **Map before mining**: Understanding what exists and where it lives prevents wasted effort searching in barren areas and missing rich veins of information.
2. **Information is unevenly distributed**: Some aspects of any topic are over-documented while others are almost invisible. The map should make this asymmetry explicit.
3. **Source ecosystems differ**: Academic research, industry reports, government data, journalism, and practitioner knowledge follow different publication norms, access models, and quality standards.
4. **Absence is information**: Documenting where information does not exist is as valuable as documenting where it does. Gaps are findings that must be reported, not hidden.
5. **The landscape changes**: Information availability evolves as new research is published, data is released, or sources go offline. Maps have a shelf life and should note their date of construction.

## Step-by-Step Methodology

### Step 1: Define the Topic Boundaries
State the research topic and its key sub-topics or dimensions. Key question: What are the distinct facets of this topic that might have separate information ecosystems?

### Step 2: Identify Source Ecosystems
List the types of sources that might contain relevant information: academic journals, government databases, industry associations, news media, expert communities, patents, legal filings, social media. Key question: For each sub-topic, which source ecosystems are most likely to hold relevant information?

### Step 3: Conduct Reconnaissance Searches
Run broad initial searches across each source ecosystem to gauge availability. Key question: How much material exists, and is it easily accessible or behind paywalls, classification, or language barriers?

### Step 4: Assess Information Density
For each sub-topic and source ecosystem, rate information density as rich, moderate, sparse, or absent. Key question: Where is there more information than we can process, and where is there almost nothing?

### Step 5: Evaluate Information Quality Distribution
Note where high-quality rigorous sources concentrate versus where only anecdotal or low-quality sources exist. Key question: In areas with abundant information, is it predominantly high-quality or predominantly noise?

### Step 6: Identify Contested Zones
Flag sub-topics where sources actively disagree or where the information is politically, commercially, or ideologically charged. Key question: Where should we expect conflicting evidence, and what drives the disagreement?

### Step 7: Document Access Barriers
Record practical obstacles: paywalls, language, classification, proprietary data, expert access requirements. Key question: What information exists but may be impractical to obtain within project constraints?

### Step 8: Produce the Landscape Map
Synthesize findings into a visual or tabular map showing density, quality, contestation, and access for each sub-topic across source ecosystems. Key question: Where should we concentrate research effort for the highest return?

## Output Format
- Landscape matrix: sub-topics as rows, source ecosystems as columns, cells rated by density and quality
- Contested zones register with description of the nature and drivers of disagreement
- Access barrier inventory with assessment of feasibility to overcome
- Recommended research strategy: where to invest deep effort, where to accept surface-level coverage, where to acknowledge gaps
- Known unknowns list: questions where information genuinely does not appear to exist

## Integration with Other Frameworks
- **Scoping Canvas**: The landscape map informs scope decisions by revealing where research effort will be productive.
- **Search Iteration Framework**: The map guides which areas need intensive iterative searching and which are already well-mapped.
- **Source Class Routing**: The source ecosystem assessment directly informs source selection strategy.
- **Epistemic Humility**: Landscape gaps translate directly into epistemic limitations that must be declared in findings.
- **Search Architecture**: The landscape map is the primary input to designing the overall search architecture.
- **Stakeholder Needs Mapping**: Landscape gaps may require resetting stakeholder expectations about what the research can deliver.

## Anti-Patterns
- **Assuming uniform coverage**: Treating all sub-topics as equally well-documented and allocating equal research effort to each.
- **Confusing absence with insignificance**: Concluding that a sub-topic is unimportant because little information exists, when the gap may reflect access barriers or recency.
- **Mapping without acting**: Producing a landscape map but then ignoring it when allocating research time.
- **Single-ecosystem bias**: Only surveying source types familiar to the researcher while ignoring ecosystems that may hold critical information.
- **Static map assumption**: Treating the landscape map as permanent when information availability may shift during the research period.
- **Over-investing in mapping**: Spending so long mapping the landscape that insufficient time remains for actual research. The map is a means, not an end.
- **Language bias**: Mapping only English-language sources when significant information exists in other languages, particularly for global or regional topics.
