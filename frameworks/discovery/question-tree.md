# Question Tree

## Purpose
A hierarchical tree structure for decomposing complex research questions into manageable, actionable sub-questions. The root represents the overarching question, branches represent thematic sub-questions, and leaves represent specific, answerable research tasks.

## When to Use
- After completing the Scoping Canvas to break down prioritized questions
- When a research question is too broad to tackle directly
- When coordinating research across multiple investigators or agents
- When you need to ensure comprehensive coverage of a topic

## Inputs
- Prioritized research questions from the Scoping Canvas
- Domain knowledge sufficient to identify major sub-topics
- Known information gaps and areas of uncertainty
- Stakeholder requirements for depth versus breadth

## Process

### 1. Define the Root Question
Write the main research question at the top. It should be specific enough to guide research but broad enough to require decomposition. Test: Can this be answered with a single search? If yes, it is a leaf, not a root.

### 2. Identify First-Level Branches
Break the root into three to seven major sub-questions. Each branch should represent a distinct dimension of the root question:
- **Definitional**: What exactly are we talking about?
- **Causal**: What drives or causes this?
- **Comparative**: How does this compare to alternatives?
- **Temporal**: How has this changed over time?
- **Stakeholder**: Who is affected and how?

### 3. Decompose Branches into Leaves
Each branch gets further broken into specific, answerable tasks:
- Each leaf should be answerable through one to three searches or source consultations
- Leaves should specify the type of evidence needed (data, expert opinion, case study)
- Mark dependencies between leaves where one answer informs another

### 4. Assign Priorities and Sequence
- **Critical path leaves**: Must be answered first because other tasks depend on them
- **High value leaves**: Likely to yield the most important insights
- **Nice-to-have leaves**: Answer only if time permits
- **Conditional leaves**: Only pursue if a prior leaf yields a specific result

### 5. Track Progress
As each leaf is answered, mark it complete and note whether the answer triggers new branches or modifies existing ones. The tree is a living structure.

## Outputs
- A complete question tree with root, branches, and leaves
- Priority assignments for each leaf task
- Dependency map showing sequencing requirements
- A ready-to-execute research plan where each leaf is an assignable task

## Common Pitfalls
- **Going too deep too fast**: Limit the tree to three levels initially. Add depth only where needed.
- **Overlapping branches**: Ensure branches are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive (MECE). Overlap causes duplicated effort.
- **Leaves that are still too broad**: If a leaf cannot be answered in one to three searches, it needs further decomposition.
- **Ignoring dependencies**: Pursuing leaves out of order wastes time when earlier answers would have redirected later work.
- **Static trees**: Failing to update the tree as new information emerges leads to outdated research plans.
- **Balanced tree fallacy**: Not all branches need equal depth. Invest more in branches aligned with the highest-priority questions.
