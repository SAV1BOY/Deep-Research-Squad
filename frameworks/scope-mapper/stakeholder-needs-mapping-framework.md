# Stakeholder Needs Mapping Framework

## Purpose
A structured method for identifying all stakeholders who will consume or act on research findings, understanding their distinct information needs, and ensuring that research scope, depth, and output format are calibrated to serve each audience. Prevents the common failure mode of producing research that answers the wrong question for the wrong audience at the wrong level of detail. Should be completed before deep research begins.

## When to Apply
- At the start of any research project with multiple consumers of findings
- When the research requester and the ultimate decision-maker are different people
- When findings will be presented at multiple organizational levels
- When prior research was rejected or ignored due to misalignment with stakeholder expectations
- When the research topic is politically sensitive and different stakeholders have competing interests
- When scope needs to be negotiated and stakeholder priorities must inform trade-offs

## Core Principles
1. **Stakeholders are not interchangeable**: A board member, a product manager, and a technical lead need different things from the same research. Map each separately.
2. **Information needs are decision-driven**: Every stakeholder needs information to make a specific decision or take a specific action. Identify the decision first, then derive the information need.
3. **Implicit needs are the most dangerous**: Stakeholders rarely articulate all their needs upfront. Probe for unstated assumptions and expectations.
4. **Over-serving one stakeholder under-serves another**: Excessive technical depth for an executive audience wastes their time. Insufficient depth for a technical audience wastes yours.
5. **Needs evolve during research**: Initial stakeholder needs shift as early findings emerge. Build in checkpoints to reassess.

## Step-by-Step Methodology

### Step 1: Enumerate All Stakeholders
List every individual or group who will receive, review, act on, or be affected by the research output. Key question: Who will read this, who will decide based on it, and who will be impacted by those decisions?

### Step 2: Classify by Role and Influence
Categorize each stakeholder as primary (direct decision-maker), secondary (influenced by or contributing to the decision), or peripheral (needs awareness only). Key question: What power does this stakeholder have over the decisions this research informs?

### Step 3: Identify Each Stakeholder's Core Decision
For each primary and secondary stakeholder, articulate the specific decision or action the research must support. Key question: What will this person do differently based on what we find?

### Step 4: Derive Information Requirements
From each decision, work backward to determine what information is needed, at what level of detail, and with what confidence. Key question: What does this stakeholder need to know, and what level of certainty do they require?

### Step 5: Map Format and Communication Preferences
Determine how each stakeholder prefers to receive information: executive summary, detailed report, data tables, visual dashboards, or oral briefing. Key question: In what form will this stakeholder actually engage with the findings?

### Step 6: Identify Conflicts and Trade-offs
Surface cases where different stakeholders need contradictory levels of depth, framing, or emphasis. Key question: Where do stakeholder needs conflict, and how will we resolve those tensions?

### Step 7: Establish Communication Checkpoints
Define when and how stakeholders will be updated during the research process, not just at the end. Key question: Which stakeholders need interim updates to course-correct the research, and at what milestones?

### Step 8: Design the Output Architecture
Structure the research deliverable to serve all stakeholders, typically through layered outputs (executive summary, main findings, technical appendix). Key question: Can a single deliverable serve all audiences, or do we need multiple outputs?

When multiple outputs are needed, define how they relate to each other and which stakeholders receive which deliverables.

## Output Format
- Stakeholder register with name, role, classification, and core decision
- Information needs matrix mapping each stakeholder to their required depth, format, and confidence level
- Conflict log documenting tensions between stakeholder needs and resolution approach
- Output architecture showing how the final deliverable will be structured to serve all audiences
- Checkpoint schedule for reassessing needs during the research process

## Integration with Other Frameworks
- **Scoping Canvas**: Stakeholder mapping directly feeds the stakeholder and success criteria sections of the scoping canvas.
- **Question Pyramid**: Different stakeholders may prioritize different branches of the question hierarchy.
- **Executive Compression**: Output format design draws on compression techniques for executive stakeholders.
- **Layered Synthesis**: The layered output model aligns directly with multi-stakeholder delivery.
- **Decision Readiness Assessment**: Stakeholder-specific readiness thresholds depend on the needs mapped here.
- **Narrative Architecture**: The narrative structure must be designed to serve the stakeholder map.

## Anti-Patterns
- **Requester-only focus**: Treating the person who requested the research as the only stakeholder, ignoring downstream consumers and decision-makers.
- **Assumed homogeneity**: Producing a single flat document and assuming all stakeholders will extract what they need from it.
- **Need inflation**: Allowing every stakeholder to add questions without prioritization, resulting in scope that exceeds time and resource constraints.
- **Static mapping**: Mapping needs once at project start and never revisiting, even as the research direction shifts.
- **Ignoring the silent stakeholder**: Overlooking parties who did not request the research but will be directly affected by decisions made from it.
- **Format mismatch**: Delivering a 50-page report to stakeholders who needed a one-page brief, or vice versa, regardless of content quality.
- **Conflating loudness with importance**: Prioritizing the needs of the most vocal stakeholder rather than the stakeholder with the most consequential decision to make.
