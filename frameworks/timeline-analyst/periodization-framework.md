# Periodization Framework

## Purpose
A structured approach for dividing timelines into meaningful periods that reveal patterns, transitions, and causal relationships. Transforms raw chronologies into interpretive narratives by identifying the boundaries, character, and significance of distinct eras within a subject's history.

## When to Apply
- When a timeline spans multiple years or decades and needs interpretive structure
- When stakeholders need to understand "what changed and when" rather than raw dates
- When comparing the evolution of multiple entities across shared time horizons
- When identifying which historical period is most analogous to the present moment
- When a raw chronology is too dense for readers to extract meaning without grouping
- When communicating findings that require temporal context to be understood correctly

## Core Principles
1. **Periods are interpretive, not given**: Boundaries between eras are analytical choices that must be justified by evidence of meaningful change, not arbitrary calendar divisions.
2. **Continuity and change coexist**: Every period contains elements inherited from the prior era and seeds of the next. Acknowledge both stability and disruption within each era.
3. **Multiple periodizations are valid**: Different criteria (technological, political, economic) produce different but complementary period structures. No single periodization is objectively correct.
4. **Transitions matter as much as stable periods**: The intervals of shift between eras often hold the most explanatory power for understanding causation.
5. **Scale affects periodization**: The appropriate granularity of periods depends on the time horizon. A ten-year analysis needs finer divisions than a century-level view.

## Step-by-Step Methodology

### Step 1: Assemble the Raw Chronology
Gather all dated events, milestones, and data points. Key question: What events are well-established, and where are there gaps in the record?

### Step 2: Identify Candidate Boundary Events
Scan for moments where conditions shifted materially. Key question: Which events would a knowledgeable observer cite as "before and after" moments?

### Step 3: Select Periodization Criteria
Choose the dimension along which to define periods (e.g., regulatory regime, technology generation, leadership era). Key question: Which lens best serves the research question?

### Step 4: Draft Period Boundaries
Propose start and end dates for each period. Key question: Can I articulate what changed at each boundary in one sentence?

### Step 5: Characterize Each Period
Name each period and describe its dominant dynamics, key actors, and prevailing conditions. Key question: What single phrase captures the essence of this era?

### Step 6: Map Transitions
Describe the mechanism and speed of each transition. Was it sudden (shock, regulation) or gradual (demographic, cultural)? Key question: What drove the shift from one period to the next?

### Step 7: Validate Against Evidence
Check that the periodization holds up against primary data. Key question: Do events within each period behave more similarly to each other than to events in adjacent periods?

### Step 8: Test Alternative Periodizations
Apply a different lens and compare. Key question: Does an alternative periodization reveal dynamics that the primary one obscures?

## Output Format
- Period name, date range, and one-sentence characterization for each era
- Key actors, conditions, and dynamics for each period
- Transition descriptions between periods including mechanism, speed, and trigger events
- A visual timeline showing periods as labeled segments with transition zones marked
- Notes on alternative periodizations considered and why the chosen scheme was preferred
- Confidence assessment for each boundary placement (well-evidenced, approximate, or debatable)

## Integration with Other Frameworks
- **Timeline Causal Chain**: Periodization provides the macro structure; causal chains operate within and across periods.
- **Trend Inflection Framework**: Inflection points often coincide with period boundaries but may also occur mid-period, signaling internal evolution.
- **Scoping Canvas**: Periodization helps bound the temporal scope of research by identifying which periods are relevant.
- **Layered Synthesis**: Each period can become a layer in the synthesis output.
- **Counterfactual Analysis**: Period boundaries are natural points for asking "what if this transition had not occurred?"

## Anti-Patterns
- **Calendar-driven periods**: Dividing by decade or century without regard to actual shifts produces meaningless structure.
- **Single-event boundaries**: Placing a boundary at one dramatic event while ignoring that actual conditions shifted earlier or later distorts the timeline.
- **Too many periods**: More than six or seven periods in a single analysis overwhelms interpretation. Consolidate where differences are minor.
- **Presentism**: Defining periods based on what matters today rather than what mattered at the time introduces anachronistic bias.
- **Ignoring overlapping periodizations**: Forcing a single linear scheme when the subject operates on multiple independent cycles loses important nuance.
- **Teleological periodization**: Defining periods based on their outcomes rather than their contemporary conditions, making history appear more purposeful than it was.
- **Neglecting internal variation**: Treating a period as monolithic when significant regional, sectoral, or demographic variation existed within it. Always note heterogeneity within periods.
