# Timeline Turning Points Framework

## Purpose

Identify inflection points in a timeline where the trajectory of events meaningfully changed direction. Determine what caused each turning point, whether it was predictable in advance, and what its downstream consequences were.

## When to Use

- When analyzing the history of a topic to find the moments that mattered most.
- When a user asks why something changed or when the current situation began.
- When separating signal from noise in a long sequence of events.
- When evaluating whether a current moment represents a potential turning point.

## Inputs

- A chronological sequence of events, data points, or developments.
- Contextual information about the environment at each stage.
- Metrics or indicators that define the "trajectory" being tracked.
- Stakeholder perspectives on what they consider pivotal moments.

## Process

### Step 1: Trajectory Definition
Define what trajectory you are tracking. Is it market share, public opinion, policy direction, technological capability? You cannot identify a turning point without knowing what turned.

### Step 2: Baseline Mapping
Chart the trajectory over time. Identify periods of stability, gradual change, and rapid change. Use quantitative data where available, qualitative assessment where not.

### Step 3: Candidate Identification
Flag moments where the trajectory visibly shifted. Look for: sudden accelerations, reversals, plateaus after growth, growth after stagnation, or discontinuous jumps.

### Step 4: Cause Attribution
For each candidate turning point, investigate what caused the shift. Categories include:
- **External shock**: An outside event disrupted the trajectory (regulation, competitor move, natural disaster).
- **Internal threshold**: Accumulated gradual change crossed a tipping point.
- **Decision point**: A specific choice by a key actor redirected the trajectory.
- **Confluence**: Multiple factors converged at the same moment.

### Step 5: Predictability Assessment
Could each turning point have been foreseen? Evaluate:
- Were there early warning signals?
- Did any analyst or observer predict it?
- Was the underlying cause building gradually or was it truly sudden?

### Step 6: Consequence Tracing
For each confirmed turning point, trace its downstream effects. What became possible after this point that was not possible before? What was foreclosed?

### Step 7: Ranking
Rank turning points by impact magnitude and irreversibility. Some turning points redirect the trajectory permanently; others create temporary deviations.

### Step 8: Pattern Recognition
Look across turning points for recurring patterns. Do turning points in this domain tend to be caused by the same types of forces?

## Outputs

- A ranked list of turning points with dates and descriptions.
- For each turning point: cause, predictability score, and downstream consequences.
- A narrative connecting the turning points into a coherent story of change.
- Assessment of whether the current moment shows signs of being a turning point.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Hindsight bias**: Everything looks inevitable in retrospect. Assess predictability from the perspective of actors at the time.
- **Recency bias**: Overweighting recent turning points because they feel more vivid and important.
- **Great man fallacy**: Attributing turning points solely to individual decisions while ignoring structural forces.
- **False turning points**: Identifying a moment as pivotal when the trajectory was actually unchanged.
- **Missing the slow burn**: Overlooking gradual shifts that lack a dramatic single moment but are equally transformative.
- **Anchoring on the obvious**: Fixating on well-known events while missing less visible but equally important shifts.
