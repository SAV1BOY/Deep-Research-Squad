# Insight Extraction Framework

## Purpose

A systematic method for extracting actionable insights from raw research findings. Raw
data and findings are not insights — they are the raw material from which insights must
be deliberately extracted through pattern matching, anomaly detection, and connection
finding. This framework prevents the common failure of delivering information without
meaning or significance.

## When to Use

- After collecting and validating research findings, before synthesis
- When large volumes of data or findings need to be distilled into key takeaways
- When the research feels complete but the "so what" is not yet clear
- When stakeholders are overwhelmed by information and need clarity
- When you sense that important signals are hiding in the noise

## Inputs

- Validated and rated research findings from the evidence collection phase
- Confidence map showing reliability of each finding
- The original research questions and success criteria
- Domain knowledge and contextual understanding
- Stakeholder priorities and decision context

## Process

### 1. Pattern Matching
Scan across all findings for recurring themes, trends, and regularities.
- **Convergence patterns**: Multiple independent sources pointing to the same conclusion
- **Trend patterns**: Directional movement over time (increasing, decreasing, cyclical)
- **Correlation patterns**: Findings that move together across different domains
- **Distribution patterns**: Where findings cluster and where outliers exist
Document each pattern with supporting evidence. Rate pattern strength: strong (multiple
independent confirmations), moderate (some confirmation), weak (suggestive only).

### 2. Anomaly Detection
Actively search for what does not fit expected patterns.
- **Outliers**: Findings that deviate significantly from the central tendency
- **Absences**: Expected findings that are missing (the dog that did not bark)
- **Surprises**: Results that contradict prior assumptions or expert expectations
- **Asymmetries**: Uneven distributions where evenness was expected
Anomalies are often the most valuable insights. They reveal what the conventional
view is missing. For each anomaly, ask: is this noise, or is this signal? What
would have to be true for this anomaly to be meaningful?

### 3. Connection Finding
Look for relationships between findings that are not obvious on the surface.
- **Causal connections**: Does finding A cause or enable finding B?
- **Cross-domain connections**: Does a pattern in one area explain a pattern in another?
- **Temporal connections**: Does the sequence of events suggest a causal chain?
- **Structural connections**: Do similar structures in different contexts produce
  similar outcomes?
Map connections visually when possible. Distinguish between demonstrated connections
(supported by evidence) and hypothesized connections (plausible but unproven).

### 4. Significance Assessment
Not every pattern, anomaly, or connection is an insight. Apply the significance filter:
- **Actionability**: Does this change what someone should do? If not, it is interesting
  but not actionable — note it but do not elevate it.
- **Novelty**: Is this genuinely new understanding, or is it confirming what was already
  known? Confirming existing knowledge has value but lower priority.
- **Magnitude**: How large is the effect or implication? Small effects on large
  populations may matter more than large effects on small populations.
- **Reliability**: Is the insight based on high-confidence findings or speculation?

### 5. Insight Articulation
Write each insight as a clear, specific, actionable statement. Use the format:
"[What we found] + [Why it matters] + [What it means for the decision/action]."
Bad: "The market is competitive." Good: "Three new entrants captured 15% market
share in 18 months, indicating that the traditional barrier of distribution
partnerships is no longer protective, which means the current partnership strategy
needs reassessment."

## Outputs

- A prioritized list of insights, each articulated with the three-part format
- Supporting evidence trail for each insight (which findings, what pattern/anomaly/connection)
- Significance ratings for each insight (high/medium/low on actionability, novelty, magnitude)
- A visual map showing how insights relate to each other and to original research questions
- Identified gaps where insights are expected but not yet supported by evidence

## Common Pitfalls

- **Confusing findings with insights**: "Revenue grew 12%" is a finding. "Revenue growth
  is masking a deteriorating unit economics problem" is an insight. Findings describe
  what happened; insights explain what it means.
- **Confirmation bias in pattern matching**: You will see patterns that confirm what you
  expected. Deliberately search for disconfirming patterns with equal rigor.
- **Dismissing anomalies**: The instinct is to explain away outliers. Resist. Investigate
  them. The most important insight in a research project is often an anomaly.
- **Overconnecting**: Not everything is connected. Spurious connections are common when
  searching for them. Demand evidence for each connection, not just plausibility.
- **Insight inflation**: Labeling every observation as an "insight" dilutes the concept.
  A good research project typically yields 3-7 genuine insights, not 30.
- **Skipping the significance filter**: Without it, you will produce a list of
  observations rather than a prioritized set of actionable insights.
- **Solo extraction**: Insight extraction benefits enormously from multiple perspectives.
  If possible, have more than one person independently extract insights and then compare.
