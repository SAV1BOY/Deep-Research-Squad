# Layered Synthesis

## Purpose
A structured approach to synthesizing research findings in ascending layers of abstraction: raw facts, interpretations of those facts, implications of those interpretations, and actionable recommendations. Each layer builds on the one below, creating a clear chain from evidence to action.

## When to Use
- When transitioning from evidence gathering to deliverable creation
- When findings are numerous and need to be distilled into coherent narratives
- When the audience needs both the evidence base and the strategic implications
- When multiple researchers need to integrate their findings into a unified output

## Inputs
- Validated research findings with evidence ratings and confidence levels
- The original research questions from the Scoping Canvas and Question Tree
- Stakeholder needs and the decisions the research is meant to support
- Domain context sufficient to interpret findings meaningfully

## Process

### Layer 1: Facts
Assemble the verified, evidence-backed facts:
- State each fact plainly without interpretation
- Include the evidence grade and source for each fact
- Organize facts by theme or research question
- Remove duplicates and consolidate overlapping facts
- This layer answers: "What do we know for certain?"

### Layer 2: Interpretations
Analyze what the facts mean:
- Identify patterns across facts (trends, clusters, correlations)
- Explain causal relationships where evidence supports them
- Note where facts are ambiguous and offer the most supported reading
- Distinguish between what the data shows and what the data suggests
- This layer answers: "What do these facts mean?"

### Layer 3: Implications
Derive what follows from the interpretations:
- For each interpretation, ask: "So what? What follows from this?"
- Map implications to specific stakeholders and their concerns
- Identify both opportunities and risks
- Distinguish between near-certain implications and speculative ones
- This layer answers: "What does this mean for our situation?"

### Layer 4: Recommendations
Translate implications into actionable guidance:
- Each recommendation should trace back through implications to facts
- State the recommended action clearly and specifically
- Note the confidence level behind each recommendation
- Identify prerequisites, dependencies, and timing considerations
- This layer answers: "What should we do about it?"

### Integration
After building all four layers:
- Verify the chain: every recommendation connects to an implication, every implication to an interpretation, every interpretation to facts
- Remove any layer element that cannot be traced downward to evidence
- Flag recommendations built on lower-confidence interpretations
- Create a summary that walks the reader through the layers

## Outputs
- A four-layer synthesis document with clear separation between facts, interpretations, implications, and recommendations
- Traceability from each recommendation back to its evidence base
- A summary narrative that guides the reader through the logical chain
- Confidence indicators at each layer

## Common Pitfalls
- **Skipping layers**: Jumping from facts to recommendations without the interpretive and implication layers leaves gaps in reasoning that undermine credibility.
- **Mixing layers**: Embedding opinions in the facts layer or stating implications as if they are facts. Keep layers distinct.
- **Untethered recommendations**: Recommendations that cannot be traced back to facts are opinions, not research outputs. Every recommendation needs a chain.
- **Interpretation without alternatives**: Presenting only one interpretation when the facts support multiple readings. Acknowledge alternatives.
- **Implication blindness**: Failing to explore implications for all relevant stakeholders. Use the stakeholder list from the Scoping Canvas.
- **Layer imbalance**: Spending all effort on facts and rushing through implications and recommendations. Decision-makers need the upper layers most.
