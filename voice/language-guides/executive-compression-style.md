# Executive Compression Style Guide

## Purpose

This guide defines how to compress research findings for executive audiences.
Compression is not about removing content; it is about restructuring for rapid
decision-making. The executive gets the conclusion; the supporting evidence
is available one click deeper.

## Compression Ratios

| Source Length     | Target Length        | Ratio  |
|------------------|----------------------|--------|
| Full deep dive   | Executive brief      | 10:1   |
| Executive brief  | Slack update         | 5:1    |
| Slack update     | One-liner            | 3:1    |

## The Compression Framework

### Step 1: Extract the Decision

Ask: "What decision does this inform?" That is your lead sentence.
- Before: "After analyzing 47 sources across 3 markets, examining regulatory frameworks..."
- After: "Enter market X in Q3. The regulatory window closes in Q4."

### Step 2: Select the Top 3 Evidence Points

Executives need enough evidence to trust the conclusion, not to verify it.
Choose the three strongest data points.

### Step 3: State Confidence and Risk

One sentence for confidence level. One sentence for the primary risk.
- "Confidence: High, based on converging market and regulatory signals."
- "Primary risk: Competitor Y may announce before our entry."

### Step 4: Define the Ask

What do you need from the executive? Be explicit.
- "Decision needed: Approve $2M allocation by March 30."
- "Action requested: Escalate regulatory concern to legal."

## Compression Patterns

### The 1-3-1 Pattern

- 1 sentence: Bottom line
- 3 bullets: Supporting evidence
- 1 sentence: Recommended action

### The Traffic Light Pattern

- Green: What is going well (1-2 bullets)
- Yellow: What needs attention (1-2 bullets)
- Red: What is at risk (1-2 bullets)

### The So-What Pattern

For each finding, append the executive implication:
- "Revenue grew 15% (so: we are on track for the annual target)."
- "Competitor launched early (so: accelerate our timeline by 4 weeks)."

## Language Rules for Compression

1. No introductory paragraphs. Start with the conclusion.
2. No methodology descriptions. Reference "see full analysis" if needed.
3. No hedging chains. One confidence statement covers the entire brief.
4. Numbers over adjectives. "$2.3M" not "significant investment."
5. Active verbs. "Launch in Q3" not "A Q3 launch is recommended."

## Common Compression Mistakes

- Compressing everything equally instead of prioritizing.
- Losing confidence/uncertainty signals in compression.
- Removing context that the executive needs for the decision.
- Over-compressing: the brief becomes too cryptic to act on.
- Under-compressing: the brief is still an analysis, not a brief.

## Quality Check

- Can the executive make a decision from the first two sentences?
- Are supporting points limited to the strongest three?
- Is the ask explicit and time-bound?
- Does the compressed version preserve the confidence level accurately?
