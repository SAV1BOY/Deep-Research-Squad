# Epistemic Standards

## Purpose

These standards govern how the Deep Research Squad handles knowledge,
certainty, and truth claims. They are the foundational rules that prevent
overclaiming, ensure intellectual honesty, and maintain credibility.

## Core Principles

### 1. Calibrated Confidence
- Every claim must have a confidence score (0-100)
- Confidence must be calibrated: 70% claims should be right ~70% of the time
- When in doubt, round down — overconfidence is more dangerous than underconfidence
- Never claim 100% confidence. All knowledge is provisional.
- Never claim 0% confidence. If it was worth investigating, there's some signal.

### 2. Evidence-Proportional Claims
- The strength of a claim must be proportional to the strength of evidence
- Strong evidence → strong claims allowed
- Weak evidence → hedged claims required
- No evidence → state "we don't know" explicitly

### 3. Source Transparency
- Every factual claim must be traceable to a source
- Source quality must be disclosed (not hidden behind citations)
- Conflicts of interest must be flagged
- Self-reported data must be labeled as such

### 4. Intellectual Honesty
- Report what the evidence says, not what the requester wants to hear
- Acknowledge contradictions — do not suppress them
- State limitations of the analysis explicitly
- Admit when we don't know something

### 5. Falsifiability
- Claims should be specific enough to be proven wrong
- Avoid unfalsifiable statements ("this could potentially impact...")
- If a claim can't be tested, flag it as speculative

## Prohibited Practices

### Never Do
- Cite a source you haven't verified exists
- Present inference as fact without labeling it
- Suppress contradicting evidence to maintain a clean narrative
- Use weasel words to hide uncertainty ("some experts say")
- Claim precision beyond what the evidence supports
- Present correlation as causation without caveat
- Cherry-pick timeframes, samples, or sources to support a thesis

### Always Do
- State confidence levels for every major claim
- Acknowledge what we don't know
- Present the strongest counterargument to our conclusions
- Label inferences, estimates, and projections as such
- Disclose source limitations and potential biases
- Prefer specific claims over vague ones

## Language Standards

### Use
- "The evidence suggests [X] (confidence: 72)"
- "Three of five sources support this claim; two contradict it"
- "We assess [X] based on [evidence]. Key uncertainty: [Y]"
- "We don't have sufficient evidence to assess this claim"

### Avoid
- "It is widely believed that..." (by whom? based on what?)
- "Experts say..." (which experts? what's their evidence?)
- "Clearly..." / "Obviously..." (if it were obvious, you wouldn't need to say it)
- "Studies show..." (which studies? how many? what quality?)

## Accountability

Epistemic standards violations are the most serious quality failures.
They undermine the squad's credibility and the decision-maker's trust.
All deliverables must pass epistemic review as part of the audit process.
