# Wack - Scenarios: Shell's Experience

## Historical Context
Pierre Wack led Royal Dutch Shell's scenario planning team in the 1970s. Shell's scenario planning anticipated the 1973 oil crisis when competitors did not, becoming the most famous case of corporate scenario planning.

## Wack's Key Insights

### Scenarios Must Change Mental Models
- The goal is not to produce a report; it is to change how decision makers think
- "Gentle art of re-perceiving" — helping people see reality differently
- Scenarios fail if they do not alter the mental maps of their audience
- The scenario planner's job is educational, not predictive

### Predetermined Elements
- Forces that have already occurred but whose consequences have not yet unfolded
- "The future is already here, just unevenly distributed"
- Demographics (a 25-year-old today will be 45 in 20 years)
- Infrastructure investments already committed
- Regulatory changes already enacted but not yet effective
- **Key practice**: Separate what is certain from what is uncertain

### Critical Uncertainties
- Forces whose outcome genuinely cannot be determined
- Usually involve human choice, innovation, or geopolitics
- These differentiate one scenario from another
- Focus analytical energy here

## Shell's Scenario Planning Process

### First Generation Scenarios (Wack)
1. Analyze the business environment systematically
2. Identify predetermined elements (include in all scenarios)
3. Identify critical uncertainties
4. Build a few (2-3) coherent, challenging scenarios
5. Derive implications for strategy
6. Communicate in a way that changes thinking

### Lessons from Shell's Experience

#### What Worked
- Anticipating structural changes in oil markets
- Preparing decision makers psychologically for discontinuity
- Building organizational agility before crises hit
- Creating shared language for discussing uncertainty

#### What Did Not Work (Initially)
- First scenarios were too complex (simplified later)
- Scenario team was too isolated from decision makers
- Reports alone did not change mental models
- Required ongoing dialogue, not just presentations

## Scenario Communication Principles

### Make It Personal
- Connect scenarios to the daily reality of decision makers
- Use concrete examples, not abstract trends
- Ask: "What does this mean for YOUR business unit?"

### Challenge but Do Not Threaten
- Scenarios should stretch thinking, not paralyze
- Show that alternatives exist (agency, not helplessness)
- Frame as preparation, not doom

### Use Narrative
- Stories are more memorable than data tables
- Name scenarios vividly
- Create characters and events within scenarios
- Make the journey from present to future vivid

## The "Remarkable People" Method
- Interview remarkable people: mavericks, contrarians, edge thinkers
- They see what mainstream analysts miss
- Their perspectives seed scenario elements
- Not for prediction, but for perception expansion

## Key Quantitative Practices
- Always quantify scenarios (GDP, prices, volumes)
- Use consistent frameworks across scenarios
- Make assumptions explicit and testable
- Track leading indicators against each scenario

## Application to Deep Research
- Focus on predetermined elements first (what is already certain?)
- Separate certainties from uncertainties explicitly
- Build scenarios that challenge the client's mental model
- Communicate through narrative, not just analysis
- Quantify scenarios to make them concrete and testable
- Seek out contrarian perspectives to expand scenario range
- Remember: the purpose is changed thinking, not accurate prediction
