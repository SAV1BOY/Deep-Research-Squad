# Popper - The Logic of Scientific Discovery

## Core Thesis: Falsifiability
A theory is scientific if and only if it can be proven false. The goal of science is not to verify theories but to attempt to falsify them.

## Key Principles

### Falsifiability Criterion
- A statement is scientific if there exists a possible observation that would prove it wrong
- "All swans are white" is scientific (finding one black swan disproves it)
- "Everything happens for a reason" is not scientific (nothing can disprove it)
- The more falsifiable a theory, the more scientific content it has

### Verification vs Falsification
- **Verification (wrong approach)**: Seeking confirming evidence (always possible)
- **Falsification (right approach)**: Seeking disconfirming evidence (more informative)
- No amount of confirming evidence proves a universal statement
- One disconfirming instance disproves a universal statement

### Degrees of Falsifiability
- More specific predictions are more falsifiable (better science)
- Vague claims are harder to falsify (weaker science)
- "The market will go up or down" is unfalsifiable (says nothing)
- "The market will drop 15% in Q2" is highly falsifiable (says a lot)

## The Problem of Induction
- We cannot logically justify generalizing from past observations to future events
- "The sun has risen every day" does not prove it will rise tomorrow
- All knowledge is provisional and subject to revision
- Science progresses by eliminating errors, not by accumulating truths

## Corroboration vs Confirmation
- Theories are never confirmed, only corroborated (so far not falsified)
- A theory that has survived many severe tests is well-corroborated
- Corroboration is not a measure of probability of truth
- It is a measure of how well the theory has withstood attempts to disprove it

## Bold Conjectures
- Science advances by proposing bold, risky theories
- Play it safe = low information content
- The best theories make surprising predictions that could easily fail
- When a bold prediction succeeds, the theory gains significant corroboration

## Application to Research

### Evaluating Claims
1. Is the claim falsifiable? If not, it has no scientific value
2. What evidence would disprove this claim?
3. Has anyone tried to disprove it? With what result?
4. How specific is the prediction? (More specific = more scientific)
5. Are proponents seeking disconfirmation or only confirmation?

### Research Process
1. Formulate bold, specific hypotheses
2. Design tests that could falsify them
3. Actively seek disconfirming evidence
4. When evidence contradicts your hypothesis, revise or abandon it
5. Prefer well-corroborated theories over popular but untested ones

### Red Flags for Unfalsifiable Claims
- "It will happen eventually" (no time bound)
- "There are many factors" (post-hoc escape clause)
- "The data is being suppressed" (unfalsifiable conspiracy)
- "You need to believe it to see it" (confirmation bias built in)
- Ad hoc modifications to save a theory from disconfirmation

## Application to Deep Research
- Always ask: "What would prove this conclusion wrong?"
- Seek disconfirming evidence with the same vigor as confirming evidence
- Make predictions specific enough to be testable
- Rate the falsifiability of key claims in your analysis
- Prefer well-tested frameworks over fashionable untested ones
- Treat all conclusions as provisional, subject to new evidence
