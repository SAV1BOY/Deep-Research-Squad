# Booth, Colomb & Williams - The Craft of Research

## Formulating Research Questions

### From Topic to Question to Problem
1. **Topic**: "I am studying X" (too broad)
2. **Question**: "because I want to find out Y" (focused)
3. **Significance**: "in order to help readers understand Z" (why it matters)

### Good Research Questions
- Specific enough to answer
- Broad enough to matter
- Not already answered definitively
- Answerable with available evidence

### Question Hierarchy
- **Conceptual**: What do we need to understand?
- **Practical**: What do we need to do?
- **Applied**: How do we implement what we know?

## Finding and Evaluating Sources

### Source Types by Reliability
1. **Primary**: Original data, documents, firsthand accounts
2. **Secondary**: Analysis of primary sources (journal articles, books)
3. **Tertiary**: Summaries of secondary sources (encyclopedias, textbooks)

### Evaluating Sources - The CARS Checklist
- **Credibility**: Author qualifications, publisher reputation
- **Accuracy**: Supported by evidence, consistent with other sources
- **Reasonableness**: Fair, balanced, no conflicts of interest
- **Support**: References provided, corroborated by other sources

## Building Arguments

### Claim-Reason-Evidence Structure
1. **Claim**: What you want readers to believe
2. **Reasons**: Why they should believe it
3. **Evidence**: Data supporting each reason
4. **Acknowledgment**: Address counterarguments
5. **Warrant**: Principle connecting reason to claim

### Argument Quality Checklist
- Is the claim clear and specific?
- Are reasons relevant and sufficient?
- Is evidence accurate and from reliable sources?
- Are counterarguments acknowledged honestly?
- Are warrants made explicit when needed?

## Practical Research Workflow
1. Define the problem and its significance
2. Draft initial research questions
3. Conduct preliminary source review
4. Refine questions based on available evidence
5. Gather and evaluate evidence systematically
6. Build argument from evidence
7. Address counterarguments
8. Revise claims to match evidence strength

## Common Argument Fallacies to Avoid
- Circular reasoning (claim restates the evidence)
- Hasty generalization (too little evidence)
- False dichotomy (only two options presented)
- Straw man (misrepresenting opposing views)
- Appeal to authority without evidence
- Post hoc ergo propter hoc (correlation as causation)

## Application to Deep Research
- Always structure findings as claim + evidence + warrant
- Rate source quality explicitly in your research process
- Separate what you know from what you think you know
- Make the significance of findings explicit for the reader
