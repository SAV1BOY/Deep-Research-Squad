# Meta-Research Papers

## Research About Research: Key Findings

### Ioannidis (2005) - Why Most Published Research Findings Are False
- **Citation**: Ioannidis, J.P.A. (2005). *PLoS Medicine*, 2(8), e124
- Small studies, small effect sizes, and flexible designs increase false positives
- When prior probability is low, most "positive" findings are false
- **Implication**: Never rely on single studies; look for replicated findings

### Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn (2011) - False-Positive Psychology
- **Citation**: Simmons, J.P. et al. (2011). *Psychological Science*, 22(11), 1359-1366
- Researcher degrees of freedom inflate false-positive rates to >60%
- Demonstrated that p-hacking can "prove" impossible effects
- **Implication**: Scrutinize methodological choices in studies you cite

### Open Science Collaboration (2015) - Reproducibility of Psychology
- **Citation**: Open Science Collaboration (2015). *Science*, 349(6251), aac4716
- Only 36-47% of 100 psychology studies successfully replicated
- Effect sizes in replications were half the original on average
- **Implication**: Treat social science findings with appropriate uncertainty

### Fanelli (2009) - How Many Scientists Fabricate Data?
- **Citation**: Fanelli, D. (2009). *PLoS ONE*, 4(5), e5738
- ~2% admit to fabrication; 33.7% admit to questionable research practices
- True rates likely higher due to social desirability bias
- **Implication**: Cross-reference extraordinary claims across independent sources

### Ioannidis (2016) - Mass Production of Redundant Systematic Reviews
- **Citation**: Ioannidis, J.P.A. (2016). *Milbank Quarterly*, 94(3), 485-514
- Even systematic reviews can be biased, redundant, or industry-influenced
- Quality varies enormously across published reviews
- **Implication**: Evaluate review quality, not just study-level quality

### Nuzzo (2014) - Statistical Errors
- **Citation**: Nuzzo, R. (2014). *Nature*, 506, 150-152
- P-values are widely misunderstood and misused
- Many researchers cannot correctly define what a p-value means
- **Implication**: Look for effect sizes and confidence intervals, not just p-values

### Begley & Ellis (2012) - Drug Target Validation
- **Citation**: Begley, C.G. & Ellis, L.M. (2012). *Nature*, 483, 531-533
- Only 6 of 53 "landmark" preclinical cancer studies could be replicated
- Scientific community was shocked by the low replication rate
- **Implication**: Even high-impact findings may not replicate

### Camerer et al. (2016) - Economics Replication
- **Citation**: Camerer, C.F. et al. (2016). *Science*, 351(6280), 1433-1436
- 61% of 18 economics studies replicated (better than psychology)
- Effect sizes were 66% of originals on average
- **Implication**: Economics is somewhat more reliable but still imperfect

## Meta-Research Lessons for Research Agents
- **Prefer replicated findings** over single groundbreaking studies
- **Check for pre-registration** of studies when possible
- **Assess funding sources** for potential conflicts of interest
- **Look at effect sizes**, not just statistical significance
- **Be skeptical of "too clean" results** - real data is messy
- **Triangulate across methods** - converging evidence from different approaches is strongest
- **Note publication bias** - negative results are underreported
- **Weight meta-analyses** but check their quality and independence

## Application to Deep Research
- Apply a replication discount: reduce confidence in unreplicated findings
- Prefer large-sample studies over small-sample studies
- Check whether key claims have been independently verified
- Look for pre-registered studies as higher-quality evidence
- Be especially skeptical of surprising findings in low-powered fields
- Document the evidence quality behind each major conclusion
