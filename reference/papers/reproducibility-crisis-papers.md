# Reproducibility Crisis Papers

## The Replication Crisis: Key Literature

### Defining the Problem

#### Baker (2016) - 1,500 Scientists Lift the Lid on Reproducibility
- **Citation**: Baker, M. (2016). *Nature*, 533, 452-454
- 70% of researchers failed to reproduce others' experiments
- 50% failed to reproduce their own experiments
- Cross-disciplinary survey: problem is universal, not field-specific

#### Open Science Collaboration (2015) - Estimating Reproducibility
- **Citation**: OSC (2015). *Science*, 349(6251), aac4716
- Replicated 100 studies from top psychology journals
- Only 36-39% replicated successfully
- Average effect size dropped by half in replications

#### Camerer et al. (2018) - Social Science Replicability
- **Citation**: Camerer, C.F. et al. (2018). *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2, 637-644
- 13 of 21 social science experiments replicated (62%)
- High-profile experiments from Nature and Science

### Field-Specific Evidence

#### Medicine and Biology
- Begley & Ellis (2012): Only 6/53 landmark oncology studies confirmed
- Errington et al. (2021): 46% of cancer biology effects replicated; effect sizes 85% smaller
- Prinz et al. (2011): Only 25% of preclinical studies confirmed
- Estimated $28 billion/year wasted on irreproducible biomedical research

#### Psychology
- Klein et al. (2018) Many Labs 2: 14/28 classic findings replicated across 125 labs
- Effect sizes consistently smaller in replications
- Social psychology particularly affected

#### Economics
- Camerer et al. (2016): 61% of 18 studies replicated
- Chang & Li (2017): 49% of macro economics papers replicated

### Root Causes

#### P-Hacking - Head et al. (2015)
- **Citation**: Head, M.L. et al. (2015). *PLoS Biology*, 13(3), e1002106
- Suspicious clustering of p-values just below 0.05
- Evidence of widespread selective reporting across fields

#### Garden of Forking Paths - Gelman & Loken (2014)
- Researcher degrees of freedom create many possible analyses
- Even without intentional p-hacking, flexibility inflates false positives
- Data-contingent decisions happen unconsciously

#### Publication Bias - Franco, Malhotra & Simonovits (2014)
- **Citation**: Franco, A. et al. (2014). *Science*, 345(6203), 1502-1505
- Strong results: 62% published. Null results: 21% published
- Published literature is a systematically biased sample

#### HARKing (Hypothesizing After Results are Known)
- Presenting post-hoc findings as if they were predicted
- Makes exploratory research look confirmatory
- Inflates apparent prediction accuracy

### Solutions and Reforms

#### Pre-Registration
- Register hypotheses and analysis plans before data collection
- Eliminates p-hacking and HARKing
- Registered Reports: peer review before results exist
- Adoption growing but still not standard

#### Open Science Framework
- Share data, code, and materials openly
- Pre-registration platform
- Enables independent verification

#### Multi-Site Replications
- Large-scale, pre-registered replications across many labs
- Many Analysts projects: same data, independent teams
- Provides robust estimates of true effect sizes

#### Statistical Reforms
- Report effect sizes and confidence intervals, not just p-values
- Use Bayesian methods for evidence quantification
- Redefine threshold (p < 0.005 proposed by Benjamin et al. 2018)

## Application to Deep Research
- Treat unreplicated findings with explicit uncertainty
- Prefer findings from pre-registered studies
- Weight multi-site studies over single-site studies
- Check if findings survived large-scale replication attempts
- Be aware that published literature overrepresents positive findings
- Apply extra scrutiny to surprising or counterintuitive findings
- Note which fields have higher/lower replication rates
- Document evidence quality ratings for every major claim
