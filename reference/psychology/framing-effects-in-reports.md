# Framing Effects in Research Reports

## Definition
Framing effects occur when the way information is presented (framed) influences judgment and decision-making, even when the underlying facts are identical.

## Types of Framing

### Gain vs Loss Framing
- "90% survival rate" vs "10% mortality rate" (same data, different reactions)
- "70% of customers retained" vs "30% churn rate"
- Loss framing triggers stronger emotional response (loss aversion)
- **Impact**: Readers react differently to identical findings based on presentation

### Attribute Framing
- "95% lean beef" vs "5% fat beef"
- "Market leader with 35% share" vs "65% of market held by competitors"
- Positive attribute framing increases perceived quality
- **Impact**: How you describe a metric changes how it is evaluated

### Goal Framing
- "If you adopt this strategy, you will gain X" (achieve gain)
- "If you do not adopt this strategy, you will lose Y" (avoid loss)
- Loss-framed goals are more motivating for risk-averse audiences
- **Impact**: How recommendations are framed affects adoption

### Reference Point Framing
- "Revenue grew 15% year-over-year" (strong growth)
- "Revenue grew 15%, below the 25% industry average" (underperformance)
- Same number, different reference point, different conclusion
- **Impact**: Choice of comparison benchmark shapes interpretation

## Framing in Research Reports

### Data Presentation
- Absolute vs relative numbers: "2x increase" vs "from 1% to 2%"
- Time period selection: choosing start/end dates that support narrative
- Scale manipulation: zoomed-in charts exaggerate small changes
- Aggregation level: aggregate data vs segmented data tell different stories

### Finding Presentation
- Emphasizing strengths vs weaknesses of a company
- Leading with opportunities vs threats
- Ordering findings (primacy effect: first findings anchor)
- Using active vs passive voice for agency attribution

### Recommendation Framing
- Urgency framing: "Act now or..." vs "Consider when ready..."
- Certainty framing: "Will lead to" vs "May contribute to"
- Scope framing: Individual impact vs systemic impact

## Examples of Misleading Framing
- "Market grew $10B" (sounds large) vs "Market grew 0.5%" (sounds small)
- "Only 3 competitors" (concentrated) vs "3 well-funded competitors" (threatening)
- Truncated Y-axis making small changes look dramatic
- Cherry-picked time periods to show growth or decline

## Debiasing Strategies

### For Report Writers
1. Present data in multiple frames (both gain and loss, absolute and relative)
2. Use consistent reference points and benchmarks
3. Start Y-axes at zero for bar charts; label clearly for line charts
4. Report both absolute numbers and percentages
5. Acknowledge how framing choices affect interpretation

### For Report Readers
6. Reframe findings in the opposite direction: does the conclusion change?
7. Check the reference point: what is the comparison baseline?
8. Look at absolute numbers when only percentages are given (and vice versa)
9. Check time period selection: is it representative?
10. Ask: What framing would make this finding look different?

## Application to Deep Research
- Always present findings in multiple frames
- Use both absolute and relative metrics
- Be transparent about reference point choices
- Include both positive and negative framings of key findings
- Flag when a particular framing might mislead the reader
- Provide context that prevents misinterpretation
