# Availability Heuristic in Research

## Definition
The availability heuristic is the tendency to judge the probability or frequency of events based on how easily examples come to mind, rather than on actual statistical data.

## How It Works
- Events that are vivid, recent, or emotionally charged are more "available" in memory
- Greater availability is mistaken for greater frequency or probability
- This creates systematic distortions in risk assessment and judgment

## What Makes Information More Available
- **Recency**: Events that happened recently are recalled more easily
- **Vividness**: Dramatic, emotional, or sensory-rich events stick in memory
- **Personal experience**: Events you witnessed personally feel more common
- **Media coverage**: Heavily reported events seem more frequent
- **Distinctiveness**: Unusual events are more memorable than routine ones

## Availability Heuristic in Research Contexts

### Trend Assessment
- Recently published studies dominate analysis
- Dramatic market events overshadow gradual trends
- Personal experience with a technology biases assessment of its maturity
- **Counter**: Use systematic literature searches, not memory-based recall

### Risk Assessment
- Overestimate risks that are vivid (terrorism, data breaches)
- Underestimate risks that are gradual (market erosion, technical debt)
- Recent events distort probability estimates
- **Counter**: Use actuarial data and base rates for risk assessment

### Competitive Analysis
- Well-known companies receive disproportionate attention
- Smaller or quieter competitors are overlooked
- Companies with recent PR successes seem stronger than they are
- **Counter**: Use systematic competitor scanning; include lesser-known players

### Source Selection
- Familiar sources are overweighted (authors you know, publications you read)
- Novel or unfamiliar sources are underweighted
- Sources in your language or culture feel more authoritative
- **Counter**: Use diverse source databases; search outside your usual sources

## Real-World Examples
- Plane crashes vs car accidents (planes feel more dangerous despite statistics)
- Shark attacks vs vending machine deaths (sharks vastly overestimated)
- Startup success stories vs failure rates (survivorship + availability)
- AI capabilities exaggerated by vivid demos vs mundane daily performance

## Debiasing Strategies

### Systematic Data Collection
1. Use structured searches, not memory-based source recall
2. Define search criteria before searching (prevent ad hoc selection)
3. Include base rate data in all risk and probability assessments
4. Track actual frequencies rather than relying on impressions

### Balanced Evidence Review
5. Actively seek routine, boring evidence alongside dramatic examples
6. Include historical data to counter recency bias
7. Search for failures as actively as successes
8. Diversify information sources beyond familiar channels

### Awareness Practices
9. Ask: "Am I judging frequency by ease of recall?"
10. Ask: "What evidence would I be unlikely to remember?"
11. Ask: "What data exists beyond what I can easily think of?"

## Application to Deep Research
- Use systematic search strategies, not memory, for evidence gathering
- Include base rates for any probability or frequency claims
- Actively seek evidence from outside your usual information sources
- Weight gradual trends as heavily as dramatic events
- Document what you searched for but could not find (absence of evidence)
