# Authority Bias in Citations and Research

## Definition
Authority bias is the tendency to attribute greater accuracy and credibility to opinions of authority figures, regardless of the actual quality of their evidence or reasoning.

## How It Manifests in Research

### Source Credibility
- Studies from prestigious institutions receive more citations
- Papers in high-impact journals are assumed to be higher quality
- Well-known authors' claims are less critically examined
- Prestigious affiliations substitute for rigorous evaluation

### Expert Opinion Deference
- Expert opinions treated as evidence rather than informed speculation
- Credentials substitute for data in decision-making
- "Leading expert says X" carries more weight than "Data shows X"
- Contrarian experts are dismissed; mainstream experts are accepted uncritically

### Institutional Authority
- Reports from McKinsey, Goldman Sachs, or BCG assumed to be rigorous
- Government statistics assumed to be accurate without verification
- Academic consensus treated as settled truth despite ongoing debate
- Industry leaders' predictions overweighted relative to their track record

## Why Authority Bias Is Dangerous

### In Evidence Assessment
- Weak evidence from prestigious sources weighted more than strong evidence from unknown sources
- Citation networks create echo chambers around authoritative voices
- Errors by authorities propagate widely because they are less likely to be questioned
- Novel findings from non-authoritative sources are systematically discounted

### In Forecasting
- Expert predictions are no better than informed laypeople in many domains
- Philip Tetlock: average expert was roughly as accurate as a dart-throwing chimpanzee
- Yet expert forecasts are treated with high confidence
- Credentials signal domain knowledge but not forecasting ability

## When Authority IS Appropriate
- When the authority's claims are within their domain of expertise
- When their claims are based on systematic evidence, not just opinion
- When the domain is one where expertise demonstrably improves accuracy
- When multiple independent authorities agree (convergent expert opinion)

## When Authority Should Be Questioned
- Claims outside the expert's domain of expertise
- Opinions without supporting evidence or methodology
- Predictions in low-validity environments (politics, markets, technology)
- When financial or reputational incentives may bias the expert
- When there is significant disagreement among authorities

## Debiasing Strategies
1. **Evaluate evidence, not credentials**: Focus on methodology and data quality
2. **Blind review**: Assess conclusions without knowing the source
3. **Check track record**: Has this authority been accurate before?
4. **Seek diverse authorities**: Do independent experts agree?
5. **Separate fact from opinion**: Even experts speculate
6. **Apply the same critical standards to all sources**: Prestigious or not
7. **Weight evidence quality over source prestige**: A rigorous study from an unknown lab outweighs a weak study from a famous one

## Application to Deep Research
- Evaluate evidence quality independent of source prestige
- Do not let brand names (McKinsey, Harvard, Gartner) substitute for critical evaluation
- Check whether expert claims are within their domain of expertise
- Apply the same evaluation criteria to all sources regardless of authority
- Document why a source is credible (methodology, data) not just who it is
- Be especially critical of widely cited but weakly evidenced claims
