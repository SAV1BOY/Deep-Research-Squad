# Confirmation Bias Deep Dive

## Definition
Confirmation bias is the tendency to search for, interpret, favor, and recall information in a way that confirms one's pre-existing beliefs or hypotheses. It is arguably the single most dangerous bias in research.

## Manifestations in Research

### 1. Biased Search (Selective Evidence Gathering)
- Searching only sources likely to support your hypothesis
- Using search terms that presuppose a particular answer
- Stopping the search once confirming evidence is found
- Ignoring databases or sources that might contain contradicting evidence
- **Example**: Searching "benefits of remote work" instead of "effects of remote work"

### 2. Biased Interpretation
- Interpreting ambiguous evidence as supporting your position
- Applying stricter scrutiny to disconfirming evidence than confirming evidence
- Dismissing contradicting evidence as "outliers" or "methodologically flawed"
- Accepting confirming evidence at face value without critical evaluation
- **Example**: Reading a mixed study and highlighting only the supportive data

### 3. Biased Recall
- Better remembering information that supports existing beliefs
- Forgetting or minimizing contradictory evidence encountered earlier
- Reconstructing past information to fit current narrative
- **Example**: Summarizing research while unconsciously omitting contrary findings

## Empirical Evidence
- Wason (1960): 2-4-6 task - people test confirming rather than disconfirming rules
- Lord, Ross & Lepper (1979): People with opposing views on capital punishment both found the same mixed evidence supportive of their existing position
- Nickerson (1998): Comprehensive review concluding confirmation bias is "ubiquitous"
- Mercier & Sperber (2011): Argumentative theory of reasoning - our reasoning evolved for persuasion, not truth-seeking

## Why It Persists
- Cognitively efficient: confirming is easier than disconfirming
- Emotionally comfortable: avoids cognitive dissonance
- Socially rewarded: consistent positions signal competence
- Structurally embedded: algorithms and filter bubbles reinforce it

## Debiasing Strategies for Research

### Pre-Commitment
- Write down your hypothesis BEFORE searching
- Define in advance what evidence would change your mind
- Pre-register your research approach (even informally)

### Active Disconfirmation
- For every confirming source, deliberately seek one contradicting source
- Search for "[topic] criticism" or "[topic] problems" or "[topic] limitations"
- Ask: "What would someone who disagrees with this conclusion say?"

### Structural Safeguards
- Use systematic search protocols (multiple databases, documented queries)
- Apply the same quality standards to all evidence regardless of direction
- Maintain an explicit "contradicting evidence" section in your notes
- Have a "devil's advocate" review step before finalizing conclusions

### Red Team Approach
- After forming a conclusion, spend dedicated time trying to disprove it
- Steel-man the opposing position (make it as strong as possible)
- Rate your confidence on a scale and ask what would change the rating
