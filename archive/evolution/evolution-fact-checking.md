# Evolution of Fact-Checking

## Editorial → Crowdsourced → AI-Assisted

This document traces how fact-checking evolved from internal editorial
processes to AI-assisted verification at scale.

## Era 1: Editorial Fact-Checking (1920s-2000s)

### Origins
- **The New Yorker** (1927): Established the first dedicated fact-checking
  department. Every factual claim verified before publication.
- **Time Magazine**: Built systematic fact-checking as standard practice.
- **Der Spiegel**: German standard for documentation-based verification.

### Methods
- Dedicated fact-checkers on staff
- Every claim traced to primary source
- Phone calls to verify quotes
- Library research for historical claims
- Multiple rounds of checking before publication

### Strengths
- Thorough, systematic verification
- Professional standards and training
- Accountability (fact-checker's name attached)
- Prevented major errors pre-publication

### Limitations
- Expensive (dedicated staff)
- Slow (added days or weeks to publication)
- Limited to major publications
- Could not scale with information volume
- Errors still occurred (confirmation bias, deadline pressure)

## Era 2: Independent and Crowdsourced Fact-Checking (2000s-2010s)

### Key Organizations
- **Snopes** (1994): First internet fact-checking site
- **FactCheck.org** (2003): Non-partisan political fact-checking
- **PolitiFact** (2007): "Truth-O-Meter" rating system
- **Full Fact** (UK, 2009): Non-partisan British fact-checker
- **Africa Check** (2012): First dedicated African fact-checker

### Methods
- Claim identification from public discourse
- Source verification using internet resources
- Expert consultation
- Standardized rating scales (True to False)
- Published methodology for transparency

### Innovations
- **Wikipedia's model**: Crowdsourced verification with citations
- **Community Notes** (Twitter/X): Peer-reviewed context additions
- **ClaimBuster**: Automated claim detection in political speech

### Strengths
- Scaled fact-checking beyond newsrooms
- Public accountability for political claims
- Created verification infrastructure
- Raised public awareness of misinformation

### Limitations
- Reactive (checking after claims spread)
- Selection bias (what gets checked)
- Speed gap (misinformation spreads faster than corrections)
- Limited impact on beliefs (backfire effect)

## Era 3: AI-Assisted Fact-Checking (2020s-Present)

### Key Developments
- AI claim detection in real-time
- Automated source retrieval and comparison
- Knowledge graph-based verification
- Multi-source triangulation at scale
- Confidence scoring for claims

### Methods
- AI identifies checkable claims in text
- Automated search for relevant evidence
- Cross-reference against verified databases
- Generate confidence scores with evidence
- Human review for final verdict

### Strengths
- Speed: near-real-time verification possible
- Scale: can process thousands of claims simultaneously
- Consistency: same methodology applied uniformly
- Transparency: evidence chain documented automatically

### Limitations
- AI can be confidently wrong (hallucination)
- Nuance is hard (context-dependent truths)
- Novel claims may lack reference evidence
- Human judgment still essential for final verdicts

## Implications for Deep Research Squad

1. Every claim must be verified — adopt editorial-era rigor
2. Use AI-era speed for initial verification passes
3. Human review required for all high-stakes claims
4. Document the verification chain (Cochrane-style transparency)
5. Distinguish between verified facts and informed assessments
