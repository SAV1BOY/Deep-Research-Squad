# Research Failures That Mattered

## Purpose

Documenting research failures that had significant consequences. These
cases illustrate what happens when research methodology breaks down and
provide concrete lessons for avoiding similar failures.

## Case 1: Andrew Wakefield and the MMR Vaccine (1998)

### What Happened
- Published a study in The Lancet linking MMR vaccine to autism
- Sample size: 12 children. No control group. No blinding.
- Generated massive vaccine hesitancy movement

### What Went Wrong
- Tiny sample with no statistical power
- Undisclosed conflicts of interest (legal funding)
- Data manipulation discovered years later
- Peer review failed to catch fundamental flaws
- Media amplified the claim without scrutiny

### Consequences
- Millions of children unvaccinated
- Measles outbreaks in multiple countries
- Decades of public health damage
- Retracted in 2010, author lost medical license

### Lesson
Small studies with large claims require extreme scrutiny. Conflicts
of interest must be disclosed and evaluated. Replication is essential
before acting on dramatic findings.

## Case 2: Reinhart-Rogoff "Growth in a Time of Debt" (2010)

### What Happened
- Influential paper claimed GDP growth drops sharply when debt exceeds 90% of GDP
- Used by policymakers to justify austerity measures globally

### What Went Wrong
- Spreadsheet error excluded key countries from analysis
- Selective data exclusion
- Methodology not disclosed until graduate student tried to replicate
- Correlation presented as causation

### Consequences
- Influenced austerity policies in EU, US, UK during recovery from 2008 crisis
- Potentially prolonged economic suffering for millions
- When error discovered, damage was already done

### Lesson
Even top economists make spreadsheet errors. Methodology must be
transparent and replicable. Policy-relevant claims need verification
before implementation.

## Case 3: Theranos — Research as Fraud (2003-2018)

### What Happened
- Elizabeth Holmes claimed to have technology for comprehensive blood
  testing from a single finger prick
- Raised $700M, valued at $9B

### What Went Wrong
- Technology never worked as claimed
- Internal dissent was suppressed
- Board lacked technical expertise to evaluate claims
- Media hype substituted for peer review
- Due diligence was superficial

### Consequences
- Patients received incorrect medical test results
- Investors lost hundreds of millions
- Founder convicted of fraud

### Lesson
Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. Due diligence
must include independent technical evaluation. Authority and charisma
are not evidence.

## Case 4: Replication Crisis in Social Psychology (2010s)

### What Happened
- Attempts to replicate famous psychology studies failed at alarming rates
- Open Science Collaboration (2015): Only 39% of 100 studies replicated

### Key Failures
- Power posing (Carney et al.): Effect disappeared on replication
- Ego depletion: Foundational finding failed to replicate
- Priming effects: Many classic studies not replicable

### Lesson
Single studies, even published in top journals, are insufficient
evidence. Replication is essential. Effect sizes matter more than
p-values. Pre-registration prevents p-hacking.

## Meta-Lessons for Deep Research Squad

1. Never trust a single source, no matter how prestigious
2. Verify methodology, not just conclusions
3. Seek disconfirming evidence actively
4. Conflicts of interest always matter
5. Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence
6. Replication and triangulation are non-negotiable
