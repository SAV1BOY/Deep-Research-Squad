# Bias in Research — Lessons

## Purpose

Bias is the most persistent threat to research quality. This document
catalogs the most important forms of bias, real-world examples of their
impact, and strategies for mitigation.

## Cognitive Biases in Research

### 1. Confirmation Bias
**What**: Seeking, interpreting, and remembering information that confirms
existing beliefs while ignoring contradicting evidence.

**Example**: Analysts studying a company they've invested in unconsciously
favor positive data and dismiss negative signals.

**Mitigation**: Actively seek disconfirming evidence. Use pre-mortems
("assume this is wrong — why?"). Assign a contrarian analyst.

### 2. Anchoring Bias
**What**: Over-relying on the first piece of information encountered.

**Example**: First market size estimate seen (say, $50B) anchors all
subsequent analysis, even if better estimates exist.

**Mitigation**: Seek multiple independent estimates before forming a view.
Start with base rates, not specific numbers.

### 3. Survivorship Bias
**What**: Drawing conclusions from successful examples while ignoring failures.

**Example**: Studying successful startups to find success patterns while
ignoring the thousands of failed startups with the same patterns.

**Mitigation**: Actively seek failure cases. Ask "What about the ones
that didn't make it?" Include failure data in all analyses.

### 4. Authority Bias
**What**: Giving excessive weight to sources because of their prestige
rather than their evidence quality.

**Example**: Accepting McKinsey's market estimate uncritically because
it's McKinsey, even when their methodology is unclear.

**Mitigation**: Evaluate methodology, not source prestige. Our evidence
grading system rates evidence quality, not source brand.

### 5. Availability Bias
**What**: Overweighting information that comes to mind easily.

**Example**: Overestimating market risks that have been in recent news
while underestimating less-publicized but more probable risks.

**Mitigation**: Systematic source mapping ensures breadth. Base rate
analysis provides objective frequency data.

## Structural Biases

### 6. Funding Bias
Research funded by parties with financial interests tends to favor
those interests. Pharmaceutical industry-funded trials show positive
results more often than independent trials.

**Mitigation**: Always check funding sources. Flag potential conflicts.
Weight independent sources more heavily.

### 7. Publication Bias
Positive and significant results are more likely to be published.
The published literature overestimates effect sizes and underestimates
null results.

**Mitigation**: Search for grey literature, pre-prints, and unpublished
data. Note the file drawer problem in evidence assessments.

### 8. Geographic and Cultural Bias
Most research is produced in Western, English-speaking countries.
This creates blind spots about other markets and cultures.

**Mitigation**: Source diversity analyzer checks geographic diversity.
Actively seek non-Western perspectives for global topics.

## Bias Detection Checklist

For every research deliverable, check:
- [ ] Have we sought disconfirming evidence?
- [ ] Are there funding or incentive conflicts in our sources?
- [ ] Are we anchored on an early estimate?
- [ ] Have we included failure cases, not just successes?
- [ ] Are we weighting evidence by quality or by source prestige?
- [ ] Have we checked geographic and cultural diversity?
- [ ] Would a skeptic find our evidence persuasive?
- [ ] Have we run a pre-mortem on our conclusions?

## Key Principle

Bias cannot be eliminated — only managed. The goal is not bias-free
research (impossible) but bias-aware research where limitations are
transparent and mitigations are built into the process.
